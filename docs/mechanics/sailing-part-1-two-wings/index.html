Sailing, Part 1: Two Wings in Balance

Why a sailboat moves, how far it heels and how close to the wind it can point, worked out for a real 35-foot racing yacht and checked against 43 of its rating certificates

A sailing keelboat is two wings, one in air and one in water, held in balance in three dimensions. This post builds a small velocity prediction program for the J/109 from published sail and hull data, solves the balance of drive, side force and heel at every wind speed and angle, checks it against the boat’s ORC certificates, and explains the words sailors use for all of it.

A sailing yacht is two wings. One, the sails, works in air. The other, the keel, works in water. The wind pushes on the sails with a force that points mostly sideways, and the keel pushes back with a force that also points mostly sideways, and what is left over, a small forward component, drives the boat. Everything else about sailing (why you cannot sail straight into the wind, why the boat leans over, why it goes fastest with the wind on the beam and slows down when the wind is directly behind) comes from how those two forces, and the twisting moment between them, balance.

This post works that balance out for a real boat, the J/109, a 35-foot racing and cruising yacht designed by Rod Johnstone and built by J/Boats since the early 2000s. It is a common boat in handicap racing, which matters here: every boat racing under the Offshore Racing Congress (ORC) rule carries a certificate listing its predicted speed at each wind speed and angle, and 43 J/109 certificates from nine countries for 2024 are public. They are the check.

A second post takes the same model through the sailing modes: the best angles upwind and downwind and why the fastest route downwind is not straight downwind, what a tack or a gybe costs, simulated second by second, and what a tidal current does to the wind a boat sails in.

How this post is built

The approach is the one used in the Apollo 11 series: documented numbers as anchors, physics between them, and a small number of quantities fitted so that the physics passes through the anchors.

  • The boat’s dimensions come from its ORC certificates (displacement, crew weight, wetted surface, draft, sail areas: the median of the 43) and from the J/109 class association (waterline length, ballast, rig dimensions).
  • The sails are described by the ORC’s published lift and drag coefficients for a mainsail, a jib and an asymmetric spinnaker, measured in wind tunnels and refined against real boats.
  • The hull’s wave-making resistance comes from the ORC’s resistance surfaces, derived from towing-tank tests of systematic series of yacht hulls; friction from the standard turbulent flat-plate line.
  • The keel and rudder are treated as wings, with lifting-line theory for their lift and induced drag.
  • Two numbers nobody publishes are fitted to the certificates: the hull’s stability (its metacentric height, \(GM\)) and its effective sailing length.

At every true wind speed and angle the program then finds the boat speed, heel and leeway at which the forces balance, and picks the sail trim that gives the most speed without heeling the boat beyond 30°.

// Bilinear interpolation in the solved grid (true wind speed every 2 knots, angle every 2 degrees).
at = {
  const TWS = M.tws, TWA = M.twa, nA = TWA.length;
  const row = (i, j) => data.grid[i * nA + j];
  return (tws, twa) => {
    const fi = Math.max(0, Math.min(TWS.length - 1.0001, (tws - TWS[0]) / (TWS[1] - TWS[0])));
    const fj = Math.max(0, Math.min(nA - 1.0001, (twa - TWA[0]) / (TWA[1] - TWA[0])));
    const i = Math.floor(fi), j = Math.floor(fj), a = fi - i, b = fj - j;
    const r00 = row(i, j), r01 = row(i, j + 1), r10 = row(i + 1, j), r11 = row(i + 1, j + 1);
    const out = {};
    for (const [k, c] of Object.entries(COL)) {
      out[k] = (1 - a) * ((1 - b) * r00[c] + b * r01[c]) + a * ((1 - b) * r10[c] + b * r11[c]);
    }
    const near = row(Math.round(fi), Math.round(fj));
    out.kite = near[COL.kite] > 0.5;
    out.tws = tws; out.twa = twa;
    out.vmg = out.v * Math.cos(twa * Math.PI / 180);
    out.epsA = Math.atan2(out.drag, out.lift) * 180 / Math.PI;
    out.epsH = Math.atan2(out.rtot, out.side) * 180 / Math.PI;
    return out;
  };
}

The wind the sails feel

A moving boat makes its own wind. If you motor at 7 knots on a calm day, you feel a 7-knot headwind. Under sail the same thing happens, and the wind the sails actually feel, the apparent wind, is the vector sum of the true wind and that headwind. With the true wind speed \(w\) at angle \(\gamma\) to the course and the boat moving at \(v\),

\[ v_a^2 = w^2 + v^2 + 2wv\cos\gamma, \qquad \tan\beta = \frac{w\sin\gamma}{w\cos\gamma + v}, \]

where \(\beta\) is the apparent wind angle. Two refinements matter. The wind is slower near the water than at the masthead, so the model scales the true wind with height using a logarithmic profile, \(w(z) \propto \ln(z/z_0)\) with roughness length \(z_0 = 5\) mm, as the ORC does. And when the boat heels, the part of the wind blowing across the boat no longer meets the sails square on: only its component in the plane of the tilted mast, \(w\sin\gamma\cos\phi\), counts.

Close-hauled in 12 knots of true wind at 38°, the J/109 sails at 6.4 knots, and its sails feel 16.7 knots at 22°: more wind than there is, from further ahead. On a run the boat’s speed is subtracted from the wind instead, which is why running in a breeze feels calm and warm, and beating into the same breeze feels cold and wet.

Wind, sea and the Beaufort scale

Sailors describe the wind by its Beaufort force, a scale drawn up by Francis Beaufort, a Royal Navy officer, in 1805 for describing the effect of the wind on a warship’s sails, and later tied to measured wind speeds and to the state of the sea. The J/109’s certificates cover forces 2 to 6:

The Met Office’s wave heights are for a sea that has had the wind blowing over it long enough, and over enough open water, to be fully developed. Most racing happens in coastal waters where the fetch, the distance of open water upwind, is much shorter, and the waves stay smaller and shorter. The standard relations for a fetch-limited wind sea, from the JONSWAP North Sea wave project, give the significant wave height \(H_s\) and the peak period \(T_p\) from the wind speed \(U\) and the fetch \(F\):

\[ \frac{g H_s}{U^2} = 1.6\times10^{-3}\left(\frac{gF}{U^2}\right)^{1/2}, \qquad \frac{g T_p}{U} = 0.286\left(\frac{gF}{U^2}\right)^{1/3}, \]

and the wavelength of deep-water waves of period \(T\) is \(gT^2/2\pi\). The 3D view uses these for 20 km of fetch, with a few wave trains travelling downwind at their own speeds and white crests from force 4, and the boat pitching, rolling and heaving on them. The waves are for show: the speed model assumes flat water. Waves slow a real boat, most of all upwind, where it climbs every one of them; that is one reason the ORC’s own program is more detailed than this one.

The sail is a wing

A sail set at a small angle to the apparent wind turns the airflow, and the turning produces a lift force at right angles to the apparent wind and a smaller drag force along it. Both scale with the dynamic pressure of the apparent wind and the sail area \(A\):

\[ L = \tfrac12 \rho_\text{air}\, v_a^2\, A\, C_L(\beta), \qquad D = \tfrac12 \rho_\text{air}\, v_a^2\, A\, C_D(\beta). \]

The coefficients \(C_L\) and \(C_D\) depend on the apparent wind angle and the type of sail. The ORC publishes them for a mainsail, a jib and several kinds of spinnaker; they are the backbone of every modern handicap rating:

Solid lines are lift, dashed drag; dots are the ORC’s published points and the lines the smooth curves through them used in the model. Close-hauled, the sails work like an aircraft wing near its best angle: high lift, little drag. Running, the flow separates, lift falls away and the sail becomes a parachute that works by drag alone.

Lift and drag are defined relative to the apparent wind, but the boat needs them relative to its course. Resolving them gives the drive along the course and the side force across it:

\[ F_\text{drive} = L\sin\beta - D\cos\beta, \qquad F_\text{side} = L\cos\beta + D\sin\beta. \]

Close to the wind \(\beta\) is small, so most of the lift becomes side force and the drag subtracts directly from the drive. In the example above the sails produce 4,190 N of lift and 725 N of drag, which turn into 926 N of drive and 4,150 N of side force: the sails push the boat sideways 4.5 times as hard as they push it forward. Turn on Lift and drag in the 3D view to see the two decompositions of the same force.

The drag here includes more than the sails’ profile drag. A finite wing sheds tip vortices, and the energy in them shows up as induced drag, \(C_{D_i} = C_L^2/(\pi\,h_\text{eff}^2/A)\), which depends on the rig’s effective height \(h_\text{eff}\), set by the ORC’s formulas from the mast height and the apparent wind angle. The mast, rigging and topsides add their own windage.

The keel is a wing too

The side force has to be resisted, or the boat would just blow sideways. It is resisted by the keel, and the keel can only do that by meeting the water at an angle, which means the boat moves slightly sideways as well as forward. That angle is the leeway, \(\lambda\). Like the sail, the keel produces lift in proportion to its angle of attack:

\[ F_\text{keel} = \tfrac12 \rho_\text{water}\, v^2\, S\, \frac{2\pi\,\mathcal{A}_e}{\mathcal{A}_e + 2}\,\lambda, \]

where \(S\) is the keel’s area and \(\mathcal{A}_e\) its effective aspect ratio. The hull acts as a mirror at the keel’s root, so \(\mathcal{A}_e\) is twice the geometric value. The rudder and the canoe body carry part of the side force too, the rudder more as the boat heels. Water is about 840 times denser than air, so the keel, at a few square metres, can balance sails of 70 m² at a leeway of only 2.7° in the example. The cost is the keel’s own induced drag, \(F^2/(\tfrac12\rho v^2\,\pi\, b_\text{eff}^2)\) for an effective span \(b_\text{eff}\), which grows as the square of the side force and falls as the square of the speed. That is why a boat sailing slowly close to the wind is so inefficient, and why a deep keel points higher than a shallow one.

Resistance: friction and waves

The drive has to equal the water’s resistance to the hull moving forward. There are three main parts:

  • Friction, from the water dragged along the hull’s wetted surface, \(R_F = \tfrac12\rho v^2 S_w C_F(\mathrm{Re})\), with the friction coefficient from the turbulent flat-plate line \(C_F = 0.066/(\log_{10}\mathrm{Re} - 2.03)^2\), and the same for the keel and rudder.
  • Wave-making resistance, the energy that goes into the bow and stern waves. It depends on the Froude number \(\mathrm{Fn} = v/\sqrt{gL}\), and on the hull’s slenderness: its length against the cube root of its displaced volume. The model takes it from the ORC’s resistance surfaces.
  • Induced drag of the keel, rudder and hull, from the side force (above).

Friction dominates in light air; above about 7 knots the waves take over, and they grow so fast that the boat runs into what sailors call hull speed. At \(\mathrm{Fn} \approx 0.4\) the wave the boat makes is as long as the boat itself, and the boat sits in the trough of its own wave with its bow climbing the face. The old rule of thumb puts this at \(1.34\sqrt{L}\) knots for a waterline \(L\) in feet: 7.7 knots for the J/109’s sailing length. It is not a hard limit, as the polar shows: in strong wind with the spinnaker up the J/109 reaches 11.8 knots, but each extra knot costs much more force than the one before.

The fit put the J/109’s effective sailing length at 10.09 m against a static waterline of 9.30 m. The boat has overhangs, particularly at the stern, which immerse as it heels and as its own wave lifts the stern, so it sails longer than it floats. The ORC rule measures the same effect.

Heel and the righting moment

The sail’s side force acts high up, at the centre of effort 7.1 m above the water in the example, and the keel’s side force acts below the water. They form a couple that tries to roll the boat over. The boat resists with a righting moment: when it heels, the centre of buoyancy moves to the low side while the centre of gravity (with 1.77 tonnes of ballast in the keel, low down) stays put, and weight and buoyancy form a couple of their own. For small angles the lever arm between them is \(GZ = GM\sin\phi\), where \(GM\) is the metacentric height. The crew, sitting on the windward rail, add their own lever:

\[ F_\text{side}\,(z_\text{CE} + 0.43\,T) = \Delta g\,GM\sin\phi + m_\text{crew}\, g\, y_\text{crew}\cos\phi. \]

The boat heels until the two moments are equal. The fit put the J/109’s \(GM\) at 1.05 m, which with 5.7 tonnes sailing displacement and 620 kg of crew on the rail gives a righting moment of 33 kN·m at 25° of heel; about 24% of that comes from the crew. Heel is costly: it tilts the sails away from the wind, reduces the keel’s effective span and increases its induced drag, and makes the hull asymmetric in the water. Racing crews keep a boat like this at or below about 25 to 30°, and the model allows at most 30°.

When the wind gets up, the boat cannot use all the force its sails could make. The model then depowers: it flattens the sails and twists their upper parts off to leeward, which reduces lift (and heeling moment) more than it reduces drive, and when that is not enough it reefs. Both are what a crew does: flattening by tensioning the backstay, outhaul and cunningham and easing the traveller, reefing by tying down the bottom of the mainsail. In the panel above, power is the fraction of the sails’ full lift being used. Up to about 10 knots of true wind the J/109 sails close-hauled at full power; beyond that it is flattening, and from about 20 knots it is reefing.

Why you cannot sail into the wind

Put the three balances together and a simple relation appears, usually credited to the aeronautical pioneer Frederick Lanchester and known in yacht design as the course theorem. The sail’s total force leans back from the perpendicular to the apparent wind by the sail’s drag angle \(\varepsilon_A = \arctan(D/L)\). The water’s total force leans back from the perpendicular to the course by the hull’s drag angle \(\varepsilon_H = \arctan(R/F_\text{keel})\). In steady sailing the two forces are equal and opposite, and the geometry forces

\[ \beta = \varepsilon_A + \varepsilon_H. \]

htl.html`<div class="sl-panel" style="font-variant-numeric:tabular-nums">Right now, at ${fmt(twa, 0)}° in ${fmt(tws, 1)} knots: sail drag angle <i>ε</i><sub>A</sub> = ${fmt(S.epsA, 1)}°, hull drag angle <i>ε</i><sub>H</sub> = ${fmt(S.epsH, 1)}°, sum ${fmt(S.epsA + S.epsH, 1)}°; apparent wind angle <i>β</i> = ${fmt(S.awa, 1)}°.</div>`

The apparent wind angle a boat can sail at is the sum of the inefficiencies of its two wings. No sail is drag-free and no hull is resistance-free, so \(\beta\) can never be zero. A J/109 needs an apparent wind angle of at least about 22°, which with its speed comes to a true wind angle in the high thirties; anything closer and the sails luff and the boat stops. That wedge either side of the wind is the no-go zone, shaded on the polar. To go upwind a boat zigzags, beating on alternate tacks. Every improvement in sailing efficiency, from better sail cloth to deeper keels, is an attack on \(\varepsilon_A\) or \(\varepsilon_H\).

The words, and where they come from

Sailing has its own vocabulary, much of it centuries old and most of it borrowed from the languages of the North Sea, where the European sailing ship developed. Dutch, the Scandinavian languages and Old English supply most of it, and because sailors’ stories travel better than their documents, it also carries more than its share of folk etymology. A few things stand out. Starboard is the steering side: Old English steorbord, the side the steering oar hung from. Early Germanic boats were steered from the right; that this was because most people are right-handed is a plausible explanation rather than a documented one. The left side was larboard until an Admiralty order of 1844 replaced it with port in the Royal Navy (the US Navy followed in 1846). The usual explanation is that larboard and starboard were too easily confused when shouted into a gale, though at least one historian of the words argues the change was simply standardisation, and why the left side was called port is itself disputed. Heel in the sense of leaning over has nothing to do with feet; it is Old English hieldan, “to lean”, which lost its final d in the 16th century. Tiller comes from weaving, through the stock of a crossbow. Yacht is Dutch jacht, short for jachtschip, a fast ship for chasing. And leeward, the side away from the wind, is still pronounced “loo-ard” by sailors.

The best stories are usually the least certain. The spinnaker is first recorded in 1866; the popular account that it is a corruption of Sphinx, a yacht that carried an early one, is not supported by the publications of the time, and the word’s origin is unknown. The genoa is named after the Italian city, but whether the sail was first shown there by the Swedish yachtsman Sven Salén, or by an Italian sailmaker a year earlier, depends on which account you read. And “port out, starboard home” as the origin of posh is a story without evidence: the acronym is only recorded decades after the word.

Method and sources

The model is a three-degree-of-freedom velocity prediction program: at each true wind speed and angle it solves for boat speed, heel and leeway so that drive equals resistance, the sails’ side force equals the side force from keel, rudder and hull, and the heeling moment equals the righting moment. The sail trim (a flattening factor scaling the lift and, through the ORC’s drag multiplier, the drag, and a reefing factor scaling the area and the height of the centre of effort) is chosen for the highest speed with at most 30° of heel. Upwind the boat carries its mainsail and jib; from 70° it may set its asymmetric spinnaker instead of the jib if that is faster. The code is in the blog’s repository, in src/sailing.

Two quantities are fitted by least squares to the median of the 43 certificates’ 64 polar speeds and their 8 best upwind VMGs: the metacentric height \(GM\) = 1.05 m and the sailing length 10.09 m. Several dimensions are assumed, because no source gives them: the waterline beam (3.00 m), the canoe-body draft (0.50 m), the keel’s and rudder’s planforms, the heights of the deck and boom above the water at the mast, and the crew’s average distance from the centreline (1.45 m). The sail shapes and sheeting in the 3D view are drawn for illustration.

What the model leaves out: waves and the motion they cause, the yaw balance between sails and rudder (weather helm), dynamic effects like surfing, and the detailed sail and hull geometry the ORC’s program uses. The ORC’s documentation describes all of these.

  • ORC certificate data for 2024, as collected by J. P. Waagmeester, orc-data (MIT licence): the 43 J/109 certificates.
  • M. Lauber, Python-VPP (MIT licence): digitised ORC coefficient tables and resistance surfaces, used here as data. The model code is independent.
  • J/109 class association, specifications: waterline length, ballast, rig dimensions.
  • L. Larsson, R. E. Eliasson and M. Orych, Principles of Yacht Design: the course theorem and the structure of a velocity prediction program.
  • Word origins: Online Etymology Dictionary, Merriam-Webster, Wordorigins.org, and the other sources linked on each term.
