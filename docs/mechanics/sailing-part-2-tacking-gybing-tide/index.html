Sailing, Part 2: Tacking, Gybing and the Tide

Why a yacht zigzags to windward and downwind, what a tack or a gybe really costs, and what a current does to the wind a boat sails in, worked out for the J/109

The second post on the physics of sailing. The J/109 model from Part 1 is used to find the best angles upwind and downwind, then extended in time to simulate tacks and gybes second by second, and finally put on moving water to show how a current changes a boat’s course, its speed over the ground and the wind it sails in.

Part 1 built a model of the J/109, a 35-foot racing yacht, that balances the forces on its sails, keel and hull at every wind speed and angle, and checked it against 43 of the boat’s rating certificates. It answers the question “how fast does the boat go on this heading?”. Sailing is about more than that. A boat that has to go upwind cannot point at its destination; one that has to go downwind usually should not; and at the corners of every zigzag it has to turn through the wind, losing speed in the process. All of this happens on water that is itself moving.

This post takes the Part 1 model through three questions:

  1. Which way to point. The best angles to the wind upwind and downwind, and why they are what they are.
  2. What a tack or a gybe costs. The model extended in time, so that the boat can turn through the wind and slow down and speed up again, second by second.
  3. What a current does. The same boat on moving water, and the real reason behind the sailor’s lore about “lee-bowing” the tide.

// Boat speed (knots) from the Part 1 polar, interpolated in wind speed and angle.
polar = {
  const TWS = M.tws, TWA = M.twa, nA = TWA.length;
  const v = (i, j) => pol.grid[i * nA + j][COL.v];
  return (tws, twa) => {
    twa = Math.abs(twa);
    const fi = Math.max(0, Math.min(TWS.length - 1.0001, (tws - TWS[0]) / (TWS[1] - TWS[0])));
    const fj = Math.max(0, Math.min(nA - 1.0001, (twa - TWA[0]) / (TWA[1] - TWA[0])));
    const i = Math.floor(fi), j = Math.floor(fj), a = fi - i, b = fj - j;
    return (1 - a) * ((1 - b) * v(i, j) + b * v(i, j + 1)) + a * ((1 - b) * v(i + 1, j) + b * v(i + 1, j + 1));
  };
}

Which way to point

What matters on a race course is rarely the speed through the water. It is how fast the boat gets towards (or away from) the wind, its velocity made good, \(\mathrm{VMG} = v\cos\gamma\), where \(\gamma\) is the true wind angle. The polar from Part 1 gives \(v\) at every angle, so finding the best angle is a one-dimensional optimisation, and it has a neat geometric form: on the polar diagram, the best upwind angle is where a line square to the wind just touches the curve.

Upwind the optimum is a compromise between speed and direction. Pointing higher than the best angle shortens the distance sailed but the boat slows faster than the angle improves; pointing lower gives more speed but sends it too far sideways. For the J/109 the best angle narrows from about 46° to the true wind in a light breeze to about 36° in a strong one, because in more wind the boat reaches its maximum speed at higher angles too, and pointing higher costs less. Upwind, the angle between the two tacks, the tacking angle, is twice that: from about 70° to 90°.

Downwind the same logic gives a less obvious answer. Running straight downwind, the boat’s own speed is subtracted from the wind: at 5.6 knots of boat speed in 12 knots of true wind, the sails feel only about 6.2 knots, from dead astern, where the spinnaker works by drag alone. Heading up 20 or 30° brings the apparent wind forward, onto the side of the sails, where they produce lift, and the speed rises more than the cosine falls. So boats downwind zigzag too, gybing between broad reaches. The best angle is 156° in 12 knots, a gybing angle of 48°. In very light wind the effect is strongest: the boat almost has to make its own wind.

These angles have a price: every zigzag needs a turn through the wind, and every turn loses speed.

The tack

In a tack the boat turns its bow through the wind, from close-hauled on one side to close-hauled on the other. For a few seconds the boat points almost straight into the wind, its sails flap and produce no drive, and it coasts on its momentum. Then the sails fill on the new side, the boat is going slowly, and it has to accelerate back to its target speed.

Part 1’s model is a steady state: it finds the speed at which the forces balance. A tack is not steady, so the model has to be extended in time. The helmsman’s turn is prescribed (the heading follows a smooth curve over a given time), and the physics does the rest:

\[ (m + m_a)\,\frac{du}{dt} = F_\text{drive} - R(u), \qquad (I + I_a)\,\ddot\phi = M_\text{heel} - M_\text{right}(\phi) - c\,\dot\phi . \]

The first is Newton’s second law along the track: the boat’s mass \(m\), plus the water it carries with it (added mass \(m_a\)), accelerates under the difference between the sail drive and the resistance, both from Part 1’s model. The second is the same for roll: the heel swings across as the sails’ heeling moment reverses, with the boat’s roll inertia \(I\) and some damping. Three more pieces make it a tack rather than just a slowdown:

  • The sails luff. Inside about 20° of apparent wind angle the sails stop producing lift and flog, adding drag. Once the bow is through the wind, the jib is briefly backed on the old side, then released and sheeted in on the new side over a few seconds.
  • The keel turns the boat. To make the boat follow a curved path, something must push it towards the centre of the turn: a centripetal force \(m u r\), where \(r\) is the rate of turn. That force comes from the keel and rudder, which need extra leeway and produce extra induced drag to supply it. A fast turn costs drag; a slow one leaves the sails luffing for longer.
  • The crew move across to the new windward side.

msample = {
  const R = mcase.rows;
  let i = d3.bisector(r => r.t).right(R, mt) - 1;
  i = Math.max(0, Math.min(R.length - 2, i));

Watch the speed trace. It falls by between a fifth and almost a half during the turn, depending on the wind and the turn, bottoms out near the end of the turn, then climbs back slowly. And watch the distance lost: in a quick turn it hardly grows at all while the boat is turning. With its sails flapping the boat still has momentum, and while its bow points into the wind that momentum carries it straight to windward, which is exactly where it is trying to go. The loss comes afterwards, in the seconds spent accelerating at well below target speed. In 12 knots of wind with an 8-second turn the J/109 takes 22 seconds to get back to 95% of its speed; in 6 knots, 39.

A tack costs the J/109 between 0.5 and 1.3 boat lengths in the model. Sailing coaches’ rules of thumb are about one boat length for a keelboat tacking in flat water (Sailing World) and one to two lengths typically (US Sailing), with more in waves. The model sits at the low end of that range, as it should: it sails on flat water, its crew never fumble a sheet, and the boat comes out of the tack exactly on its best upwind angle rather than a little low to build speed, as real crews do.

Two things in the plot are worth noticing. In light air the turn time matters little and the cost is dominated by the long, slow acceleration: there is little wind to accelerate the boat. In a breeze a quick turn is clearly better, because the slower the turn, the longer the sails spend luffing and the further the speed falls. And above about 16 knots every tack costs a little more again, because the boat comes out of the tack overpowered at low speed, with its keel at high leeway.

The gybe

In a gybe the boat turns its stern through the wind, from a broad reach on one side to a broad reach on the other. Choose Gybe above to see it. The physics is the same, but the outcome is very different, as the right-hand panel of the plot shows: a gybe costs the J/109 between a few hundredths and four tenths of a boat length, the most in light air, where the boat has least wind to accelerate it again.

The reason is that the sails never stop working. Turning through dead downwind, the apparent wind stays behind the boat, and the sails keep producing drag-driven force throughout; the mainsail’s boom swings across, and the spinnaker is gybed (its sheet released on one side and trimmed on the other) while it partly collapses for a few seconds. Nothing equivalent to the flapping sails of a tack happens, and the boat never slows much.

What a gybe costs instead is risk. The boom crosses the boat in a second or so, carrying the force of the whole mainsail, and in strong wind an uncontrolled gybe can break gear or injure anyone in its path. That is why a crew gybing in a breeze pulls the boom to the centreline first, and why sailing by the lee, with the wind on the same side as the boom, is uncomfortable: the boom can come across without warning. The model’s crew always gybe cleanly.

On moving water: current

Everything so far has assumed the water is still. In tidal waters it rarely is. A current carries the boat with it, and the physics of that is simple: the boat sails through the water exactly as it would on still water, and the water moves over the ground. Velocities just add:

\[ \mathbf{v}_\text{ground} = \mathbf{v}_\text{water} + \mathbf{c}, \]

where \(\mathbf{c}\) is the current. The surprise is what the current does to the wind. The wind a boat sails in is the air’s motion relative to the water it floats in, not to the ground. If the current carries the boat along at 2 knots, the boat feels a 2-knot breeze from the direction the current is going towards, added to the real wind. So the wind over the water is

\[ \mathbf{w}_\text{water} = \mathbf{w}_\text{ground} - \mathbf{c}, \]

a different speed and, unless the current runs exactly with or against the wind, a different direction. Sailors call the difference the tidal wind. Three cases make the physics clear.

  • Current with the wind or against it. A current running straight downwind carries both tacks the same amount the wrong way, and also takes away from the wind over the water (2 knots of current in 12 knots of wind leaves 10). Running against the wind it does the opposite: the boat is carried upwind and also gets more wind to sail in. Both tacks are affected equally.
  • Current across the wind. Now the current carries both tacks sideways by the same amount: on its own it favours neither. But the wind over the water has swung, towards the side the current comes from. With 2 knots of current across 12 knots of wind it swings by about 9°. For the tack heading into the current that is a lift, a wind shift that lets it point closer to the true upwind direction; for the other tack it is a header. In that case the favoured tack makes 5.7 knots towards the wind over the ground and the other 4.4.
  • Lee-bowing. The favoured tack is the one with the current coming against its lee bow, the bow on the side away from the wind. Sailors have long known that “lee-bowing the tide” pays, and the usual explanation is that the current pushes the boat to windward. The calculation says otherwise: the current’s push is the same on either tack. What differs is the wind the boat sails in, and the lee-bowed tack gets the lift. (A current that varies across the course, stronger in one place than another, adds real differences between tacks, which this uniform current does not.)

Current also changes the waves. Where the current runs against the wind, the waves it raises meet a current that slows them and squeezes them shorter, and they grow steeper: the short, breaking sea of wind against tide that every coastal sailor knows. Where wind and current run together the sea flattens out. The 3D views in these posts use waves for still water.

What the model leaves out

The manoeuvre model is a first step from a steady state towards a full simulation, and several of its numbers are assumed rather than measured:

  • The turn is prescribed: a smooth change of heading over 5, 8 or 12 seconds, coming out exactly on the best angle. A real helmsman steers by feel, often turning faster at the start, and comes out a little low to build speed.
  • The boat’s dynamic properties: added mass (5% of displacement in surge), roll radius of gyration (1.45 m), added roll inertia (30%) and roll damping (15% of critical), all typical values for a boat of this kind, not J/109 measurements.
  • The crew’s timing: sheets released and trimmed, and crew moving across, over 3 to 4 seconds; the spinnaker partly collapsing for 3 seconds in a gybe.
  • Flapping sails add drag with a coefficient of 0.15 on the full sail area.
  • Sideways motion is quasi-steady: the keel takes whatever leeway it needs, up to a stall at 12°.
  • Yaw is not modelled: the rudder and the sails’ turning moments, which set how fast a real boat can turn and how hard the helmsman has to work, would need yaw coefficients nobody publishes for this boat.
  • Waves are not in the physics, and they are the largest omission: in a seaway each tack costs much more, because the boat stops in the waves and has to climb them again while accelerating.

The steady-state parts (sails, hull, keel, stability) are exactly Part 1’s, so a boat sailing straight on holds its speed to within a thousandth of a knot.

Method and sources

The manoeuvre simulation integrates surge and roll with a second-order (Heun) method at 0.02-second steps, using Part 1’s sail, hull and keel forces at each step; leeway is solved quasi-statically from the side force the keel and rudder must carry, including the centripetal force of the turn. The current calculator uses Part 1’s polar, interpolated in wind speed and angle, and optimises each tack’s heading for progress over the ground. Code: src/sailing/manoeuvre.py in the blog’s repository; Part 1’s model is src/sailing/vpp.py.
