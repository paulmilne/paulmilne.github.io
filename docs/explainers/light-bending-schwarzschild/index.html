The Schwarzschild metric

In 1916, a few weeks after Einstein published his field equations, Karl Schwarzschild found their solution outside a spherical, non-rotating mass \(M\). In coordinates \((t, r, \theta, \phi)\) the spacetime interval is

\[ ds^2 = -\left(1 - \frac{2GM}{c^2 r}\right)c^2\,dt^2 + \frac{dr^2}{1 - 2GM/(c^2 r)} + r^2\left(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right). \]

The radius \(r\) is defined so that a sphere at \(r\) has area \(4\pi r^2\), and \(t\) is the time kept by a clock at rest far away. The metric becomes singular at the Schwarzschild radius \(r_s = 2GM/c^2\). That singularity is a feature of the coordinates, not of spacetime, but the sphere at \(r_s\) is real: it is the event horizon, from inside which nothing escapes.

From here on we use geometrised units, \(G = c = 1\), and measure lengths in units of \(M\) (strictly \(GM/c^2\)). The metric is then

\[ ds^2 = -\left(1 - \frac{2}{r}\right)dt^2 + \frac{dr^2}{1 - 2/r} + r^2\,d\Omega^2, \]

and the horizon is at \(r = 2\). To convert back, multiply lengths by \(GM/c^2\):

Light rays are null geodesics

A free particle follows a geodesic, a path of extremal proper time. A geodesic \(x^\mu(\lambda)\) extremises the action of the Lagrangian

\[ \mathcal{L} = \tfrac{1}{2} g_{\mu\nu}\,\dot{x}^\mu \dot{x}^\nu, \qquad \dot{x} = dx/d\lambda, \]

where \(\lambda\) is an affine parameter along the path. For light the interval vanishes, \(g_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\mu \dot x^\nu = 0\), so a light ray is a null geodesic.

The Schwarzschild metric depends on neither \(t\) nor \(\phi\), so the Euler–Lagrange equations for those coordinates give two conserved quantities:

\[ E = \left(1 - \frac{2}{r}\right)\dot t, \qquad L = r^2 \sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi . \]

\(E\) is the photon’s energy as measured at infinity, and \(L\) is its angular momentum about the axis. Spherical symmetry lets us choose the orbital plane to be the equator, \(\theta = \pi/2\). The null condition is then

\[ -\frac{E^2}{1 - 2/r} + \frac{\dot r^2}{1 - 2/r} + \frac{L^2}{r^2} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \dot r^2 = E^2 - \left(1 - \frac{2}{r}\right)\frac{L^2}{r^2}. \]

Rescaling the affine parameter rescales \(E\) and \(L\) together, so only their ratio matters for the shape of a ray. That ratio is the impact parameter

\[ b = \frac{L}{E}, \]

which, far from the hole, is the perpendicular distance between the incoming ray and a parallel line through the centre. Dividing by \(L^2\),

\[ \frac{\dot r^2}{L^2} = \frac{1}{b^2} - V(r), \qquad V(r) = \frac{1}{r^2}\left(1 - \frac{2}{r}\right). \]

This is a one-dimensional problem. A ray coming in from infinity moves inwards while \(1/b^2 > V(r)\) and turns round where \(1/b^2 = V(r)\).

The photon sphere and the critical impact parameter

\(V(r)\) has a single maximum. Setting \(V'(r) = -2/r^3 + 6/r^4 = 0\) gives

\[ r = 3, \qquad V_\text{max} = \frac{1}{27}. \]

At \(r = 3M\), the photon sphere, light can travel on a circular orbit, an unstable one, balanced on top of the potential. A ray with

\[ b_c = \frac{1}{\sqrt{V_\text{max}}} = 3\sqrt{3} \approx 5.196 \]

spirals in towards that orbit forever. This splits all incoming rays into two families:

  • \(b > 3\sqrt 3\): the ray meets the potential barrier, turns round at a radius \(r_0 > 3\), and escapes back to infinity.
  • \(b < 3\sqrt 3\): the ray passes over the barrier and falls through the horizon.

The turning point \(r_0\) and the impact parameter are related by \(1/b^2 = V(r_0)\), that is

\[ b = \frac{r_0}{\sqrt{1 - 2/r_0}}, \]

and inverting this means solving the cubic \(r_0^3 - b^2 r_0 + 2b^2 = 0\), whose largest root is \(r_0 = \tfrac{2b}{\sqrt 3}\cos\!\big[\tfrac13 \arccos(-3\sqrt3/b)\big]\). That is what S.turning_point evaluates.

The consequence for a picture is immediate. Look towards the hole from far away: every line of sight with impact parameter less than \(3\sqrt3\,M\) ends on the horizon, so the hole appears as a black disk of radius

\[ b_c = 3\sqrt{3}\,M \approx 2.6\,r_s, \]

which is more than two and a half times the size of the horizon itself. This dark disk is the black hole’s shadow. In 2019 the Event Horizon Telescope imaged the ring of light around the shadow of M87*. For its mass and distance the formula predicts an angular diameter of

Show code

which is close to the 42 ± 3 µas ring the collaboration reported. (The ring of emission sits slightly outside the shadow’s edge, so the agreement is as good as it should be.)

The orbit equation

To get the shape of a ray, trade the affine parameter for the angle \(\phi\). Using \(\dot\phi = L/r^2\) and \(u = 1/r\),

\[ \left(\frac{dr}{d\phi}\right)^2 = \frac{\dot r^2}{\dot\phi^2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \left(\frac{du}{d\phi}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{b^2} - u^2 + 2u^3 \equiv P(u). \]

Differentiating once with respect to \(\phi\) removes the constant:

\[ \boxed{\;\frac{d^2u}{d\phi^2} + u = 3u^2\;} \]

Without the right-hand side this is the equation of a straight line, \(u = \sin\phi / b\). The term \(3u^2\) (in ordinary units \(3GMu^2/c^2\)) is all of the bending. It is the same correction that makes Mercury’s orbit precess, here acting on a particle with no rest mass.

Integrating it for a fan of parallel incoming rays shows the whole story at once:

Show code 
def ray_xy(b, x0=-30.0, phi_max=4 * np.pi):
    """Integrate u'' + u = 3u^2 from a point far to the left."""
    # start on the line y = b at x = x0
    r0 = math.hypot(x0, b)
    phi0 = math.atan2(b, x0)
    u0 = 1 / r0
    # du/dphi from the straight-line approximation at the start, then exact
    du0 = -math.sqrt(max(1 / b**2 - u0**2 + 2 * u0**3, 0.0))
    def f(phi, y):
        return [y[1], -y[0] + 3 * y[0] ** 2]
    def horizon(phi, y):
        return y[0] - 0.5
    def away(phi, y):
        return y[0] - 1 / 40
    horizon.terminal = True
    away.terminal = True
    away.direction = -1
    sol = solve_ivp(f, [phi0, phi0 - phi_max], [u0, du0], events=[horizon, away],
                    max_step=0.01, rtol=1e-10, atol=1e-12)
    rr = 1 / sol.y[0]
    return rr * np.cos(sol.t), rr * np.sin(sol.t)

The exact deflection angle

A ray from infinity that turns round at \(u_0 = 1/r_0\) sweeps out an angle \(\int_0^{u_0} du/\sqrt{P(u)}\) on the way in and the same on the way out. A straight line would sweep exactly \(\pi\), so the deflection is

\[ \alpha(b) = 2\int_0^{u_0}\frac{du}{\sqrt{1/b^2 - u^2 + 2u^3}} - \pi . \]

Two ways to evaluate this are worth having, because each checks the other.

Quadrature. The integrand blows up like \((u_0 - u)^{-1/2}\) at the turning point. The substitution \(u = u_0 - s^2\) removes this, since \(du = -2s\,ds\) and \(\sqrt{P} \approx s\sqrt{-P'(u_0)}\), which leaves a smooth integrand for adaptive quadrature.

Elliptic integrals. \(P(u)\) is a cubic, \(2(u - u_1)(u - u_2)(u - u_3)\), with roots \(u_1 < 0 < u_2 = u_0 < u_3\) when \(b > 3\sqrt3\). A standard reduction (Byrd and Friedman, formula 233.00) gives, for any starting point \(y\),

\[ \int_y^{u_2}\frac{du}{\sqrt{P(u)}} = \sqrt{\frac{2}{u_3 - u_1}}\;F(\varphi, k), \qquad k^2 = \frac{u_2 - u_1}{u_3 - u_1}, \qquad \sin^2\varphi = \frac{(u_3 - u_1)(u_2 - y)}{(u_2 - u_1)(u_3 - y)}, \]

where \(F\) is the incomplete elliptic integral of the first kind. With \(y = 0\) this is half the swept angle, in closed form.

The two agree to 5e-11 radians over the whole range, from rays that graze the photon sphere to rays that pass \(10^4\,M\) away.

Far from the hole: Einstein’s 1.75 arcseconds

For \(b \gg 1\), expanding the integral in powers of \(1/b\) gives

\[ \alpha(b) = \frac{4}{b} + \frac{15\pi}{4b^2} + \frac{128}{3b^3} + O(b^{-4}). \]

Restoring units, the leading term is \(\alpha = 4GM/(c^2 b)\), twice what you get by treating light as a Newtonian particle, because space is curved as well as time. For a ray grazing the Sun this is

Show code 
R_sun = 6.957e8
alpha_sun = 4 * G * M_sun / (c**2 * R_sun) * 206264.806
print(f"4GM/(c^2 R) = {alpha_sun:.3f} arcseconds")
4GM/(c^2 R) = 1.752 arcseconds

which is the deflection the 1919 eclipse expeditions led by Eddington and Dyson set out to measure.

Close to the photon sphere: winding without limit

As \(b \to b_c\) from above, the turning point approaches the photon sphere and the ray winds round it more and more times. Near the critical value the deflection diverges logarithmically. Bozza (2002) derived the strong-field limit

\[ \alpha(b) \approx -\ln\!\left(\frac{b}{b_c} - 1\right) + \ln\!\big[216\,(7 - 4\sqrt3)\big] - \pi . \]

The coefficient of the logarithm, exactly 1 for Schwarzschild, carries a physical meaning. Each extra half-turn round the hole adds \(\pi\) to \(\alpha\). To add \(\pi\), the distance from the critical value, \(b - b_c\), must shrink by a factor of \(e^{-\pi} \approx 0.0432\). Solving \(\alpha(b) = n\pi\) exactly confirms it:

Show code 
bn = [brentq(lambda b: float(S.deflection(b)) - n * math.pi, S.B_CRIT * (1 + 1e-15), 60) for n in range(1, 7)]
print(f"{'n':>2} {'b_n':>14} {'b_n - b_c':>12} {'ratio':>8}")
prev = None
for n, bb in enumerate(bn, 1):
    d = bb - S.B_CRIT
    print(f"{n:2d} {bb:14.10f} {d:12.3e} {'' if prev is None else f'{d/prev:8.5f}'}")
    prev = d
ratio_last = (bn[-1] - S.B_CRIT) / (bn[-2] - S.B_CRIT)

The ratio settles to 0.0432, against \(e^{-\pi} = 0.0432\). The rate \(\gamma = \pi\) at which neighbouring rays peel away from the photon sphere is its Lyapunov exponent per half-orbit. The same number reappears in a picture: next to the shadow’s edge sits a stack of ever-thinner rings, the second, third and later images of the whole sky, each about 23 times thinner than the last. Only the first one or two are ever visible, but they are there.

Where this leaves us

For a non-spinning hole, everything a renderer needs is now in hand:

  • rays with \(b < 3\sqrt3\,M\) end on the horizon, which gives the shadow;
  • rays with \(b > 3\sqrt3\,M\) escape, deflected by the exact \(\alpha(b)\) above;
  • near the critical value, rays wind round the photon sphere, which gives infinite sequences of images packed against the shadow’s edge.

What is missing is the camera. These formulas describe rays from infinity seen from infinity, while Interstellar’s camera sits a few dozen \(M\) from the hole, and possibly moves. The next explainer puts the camera in place and turns \(\alpha(b)\) into images.
