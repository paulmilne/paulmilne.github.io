  1. Launch to translunar injection
  2. Translunar coast and lunar orbit

Every number in the series lives in one of about a dozen coordinate frames. This post sets out what those frames are, how one becomes another, which mathematics does the work, how a single clock keeps four panels, a 3D scene, a transcript, audio and film in step, and where the data came from and what it forced. The code is in src/apollo11 in the blog’s repository.

The map of frames

There is one working frame, and everything is either computed in it or converted to it at the edges. It is geocentric and inertial, with its axes fixed to the mean equator and equinox of 16 July 1969. The physics is integrated there. Other frames exist for two reasons: to read the 1969 documents, which were written in Earth-fixed, Moon-fixed or spacecraft-local terms, and to draw the views, each of which picks the frame where its story is easiest to see.

One clock, three time scales

Every row of every table is keyed by ground elapsed time (GET), seconds from range zero, 13:32:00 UTC on 16 July 1969. GET is the mission’s own clock: the transcripts, maneuver PADs and NASA tables all use it, so the reconstruction does too, and nothing in the pages is keyed to anything else.

Two physical clocks hang off it. Earth’s rotation follows UT1, which in July 1969 ran 0.0115 s ahead of UTC; it sets Greenwich sidereal time, and so where the launch pad and the tracking stations were. The planets follow Terrestrial Time (TT), 39.746 s ahead of UTC that day (32.184 s by definition plus 7.5622 s of accumulated leap time); it is the argument of the DE421 ephemeris and of the IAU model of the Moon’s rotation. Running Earth’s rotation on TT would misplace the launch pad by about 18 km, and running the ephemeris on UT1 would misplace the Moon by about 40 km, so each function asks for the one it needs:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{JD}_\text{UT1} &= 2440419.06388889 + (\text{GET} + 0.0115)/86400, \\ \text{JD}_\text{TT} &= 2440419.06388889 + (\text{GET} + 39.746)/86400, \\ \text{GMST} &= 67310.54841\,\text{s} + (876600\,\text{h} + 8640184.812866\,\text{s})\,T + 0.093104\,\text{s}\,T^2 - 6.2\times10^{-6}\,\text{s}\,T^3, \end{aligned} \]

with \(T = (\text{JD}_\text{UT1} - 2451545)/36525\), converted to an angle at 240 s per degree.

The working frame, and why it is 1969’s

Modern ephemerides are referred to the ICRF, which is fixed to distant quasars and is within milliarcseconds of the J2000 equator. NASA’s 1969 tables were written in the mean equator and equinox of the launch date: they give right ascension through Greenwich mean sidereal time, against an equator that had precessed by 0.43° between 1969 and 2000. Using the tables’ own frame means the anchors go in unaltered, and the only rotation applied is to the modern data, once:

\[ \mathbf r_\text{work} = P_0\,B\,\mathbf r_\text{ICRF}, \]

where \(B\) is the frame bias from the ICRF to the J2000 mean equator (milliarcseconds) and \(P_0\) is precession from J2000 to the mean equator of 16 July 1969. The ephemeris uses skyfield’s precession matrix at range zero; the Moon’s axes use the IAU 1976 angles, \(R_z(-z)\,R_y(\theta)\,R_z(-\zeta)\), cubic in Julian centuries of TT. The two agree to milliarcseconds over these 31 years.

\(P_0\) is evaluated once, at range zero. Precession over the eight-day mission is about 0.002°, a few hundred metres at the Moon’s distance, so treating the frame as inertial costs nothing the tables could show. Nutation, the 18.6-year wobble on top of precession, is left out for the same reason the 1969 tables left it out: “mean” frames exclude it by definition.

The Moon and Sun come from JPL’s DE421 through the skyfield library, rotated with \(P_0 B\) and written to ephem_1969.csv as hourly positions and velocities. That file is committed, so the engine needs only numpy. Between rows, positions are interpolated with cubic Hermite polynomials, which use the velocities as end slopes and reproduce DE421 to metres at a one-hour step.

Reading a 1969 table row

The trajectory tables in Apollo by the Numbers and the Boeing and TRW reports give a state as six numbers: geocentric latitude \(\varphi\), longitude \(\lambda\), altitude \(h\), space-fixed speed \(v\), flight-path angle \(\gamma\) (above the local horizontal) and heading \(\psi\) (east of north). Turning that into a position and velocity in the working frame is the most frequently used conversion in the project:

\[ \begin{aligned} \alpha &= \lambda + \text{GMST}(\text{GET}), \qquad |\mathbf r| = R_\text{ellipsoid}(\varphi) + h, \\ \mathbf r &= |\mathbf r|\,(\cos\varphi\cos\alpha,\ \cos\varphi\sin\alpha,\ \sin\varphi), \\ \hat{\mathbf u} &= \frac{\mathbf r}{|\mathbf r|},\quad \hat{\mathbf e} = \frac{\hat{\mathbf z}\times\hat{\mathbf u}}{|\hat{\mathbf z}\times\hat{\mathbf u}|},\quad \hat{\mathbf n} = \hat{\mathbf u}\times\hat{\mathbf e}, \\ \mathbf v &= v\,\big(\sin\gamma\,\hat{\mathbf u} + \cos\gamma\,(\cos\psi\,\hat{\mathbf n} + \sin\psi\,\hat{\mathbf e})\big). \end{aligned} \]

Two details come straight from the documents. The ellipsoid is the 1960 Fischer (“Mercury”) figure, \(a = 6378.166\) km and \(f = 1/298.3\), not WGS-84, because that is what the tables measured altitude from. And the velocity is space-fixed: it already includes Earth’s rotation, so nothing is added. The inverse, table_from_state, subtracts \(\boldsymbol\omega_\oplus\times\mathbf r\) only when it reports Earth-relative speed.

The Moon’s frames

Moon-centred inertial is the working frame with its origin moved to the Moon, \(\mathbf r - \mathbf r_\text{M}(t)\), with the Moon’s position from the Hermite-interpolated ephemeris. Its axes stay parallel to the working frame’s, so no rotation is involved, and velocities subtract the same way. Lunar orbits, perilune and apolune, and the line-of-sight geometry for loss of signal are all computed here.

Moon body-fixed turns with the Moon, and gives selenographic latitude and longitude. Its orientation comes from the IAU working group’s model (Archinal et al. 2011): the pole’s right ascension \(\alpha_0\) and declination \(\delta_0\), and the prime-meridian angle \(W\), each a polynomial in TT plus thirteen periodic terms for the Moon’s physical libration. That model approximates the Mean Earth/polar axis frame used for lunar maps and for the LRO-derived landing-site coordinates to a few hundred metres.

\[ M_{\text{body}\to\text{work}}(t) = P_0\,\big(R_z(W)\,R_x(90^\circ - \delta_0)\,R_z(90^\circ + \alpha_0)\big)^{\mathsf T}, \] \[ \mathbf b = M^{\mathsf T}(\mathbf r - \mathbf r_\text{M}),\qquad \text{lat} = \arcsin\frac{b_z}{|\mathbf b|},\qquad \text{lon} = \operatorname{atan2}(b_y, b_x). \]

Heights above the Moon need a reference, and the documents use two. The 1969 lunar tables measure altitude above a 1738.09 km sphere. The landing site lies 8,557 ft below it (TRW), so its radius is 1735.48 km; the descent’s altitudes, callouts and computer displays are all relative to that. A PDI altitude of 6.4 n mi in one source and 47,444 ft in another are the same point described from the two references.

The same matrix places the Moon’s gravity field. The model’s two lunar harmonics, \(J_2\) (flattening) and \(C_{22}\) (elongation towards Earth), are defined in body axes, so the acceleration is computed as \(M\,\mathbf a_\text{body}(M^{\mathsf T}\mathbf d)\), and only within 20,000 km of the Moon, where it matters.

Spacecraft-local frames

LVLH: the frame of every burn

Maneuver PADs read up to the crew gave each burn’s velocity change as three numbers, “Noun 81”, in a local vertical, local horizontal frame built from the spacecraft’s own position and velocity relative to whichever body it was orbiting:

\[ \hat{\mathbf Z} = -\hat{\mathbf r}\ \text{(down)},\qquad \hat{\mathbf X} = \frac{\mathbf v - (\mathbf v\cdot\hat{\mathbf r})\,\hat{\mathbf r}}{|\mathbf v - (\mathbf v\cdot\hat{\mathbf r})\,\hat{\mathbf r}|}\ \text{(forward)},\qquad \hat{\mathbf Y} = \hat{\mathbf Z}\times\hat{\mathbf X} = -\hat{\mathbf h}. \]

Impulsive burns add \(\Delta v_X\hat{\mathbf X} + \Delta v_Y\hat{\mathbf Y} + \Delta v_Z\hat{\mathbf Z}\). The six-minute lunar orbit insertion is different: the thrust direction is held in LVLH but recomputed at every integration substep as the frame turns with the spacecraft, plus a fitted pitch rate. The fit settled on 0.075°/s, almost the rate at which LVLH itself turns at that height (\(v/r \approx 0.076\)°/s), which says the real thrust stayed nearly fixed in inertial space.

Klumpp’s guidance frame

For the descent, Eagle’s computer worked in a frame centred on its landing site and rebuilt every two-second guidance cycle as the Moon turned:

\[ \hat{\mathbf X}_G = \frac{\mathbf r_\text{site}}{|\mathbf r_\text{site}|},\qquad \hat{\mathbf Z}_G = -\frac{\mathbf r_\text{LM} - (\mathbf r_\text{LM}\cdot\hat{\mathbf X}_G)\,\hat{\mathbf X}_G}{|\mathbf r_\text{LM} - (\mathbf r_\text{LM}\cdot\hat{\mathbf X}_G)\,\hat{\mathbf X}_G|},\qquad \hat{\mathbf Y}_G = \hat{\mathbf Z}_G\times\hat{\mathbf X}_G, \] \[ \mathbf r_G = C\,(\mathbf r_\text{LM} - \mathbf r_\text{site}),\qquad \mathbf v_G = C\,(\mathbf v_\text{LM} - \boldsymbol\omega_\text{M}\times\mathbf r_\text{LM}), \]

where \(C\) has rows \(\hat{\mathbf X}_G, \hat{\mathbf Y}_G, \hat{\mathbf Z}_G\). Velocity here is relative to the turning surface, which is why the Moon’s angular velocity \(\boldsymbol\omega_\text{M}\) appears. The code finds it numerically from two body matrices 60 s apart, \(W = \big(M(t+30) - M(t-30)\big)/60 \cdot M(t)^{\mathsf T}\), and reads the vector off that skew-symmetric matrix.

The site frame \((s, c, h)\)

The descent profile, the callouts and the last two minutes use distances along the surface: \(s\) downrange and \(c\) across, as great-circle arcs times the site radius, and \(h\) above the site radius. Flat-plane coordinates would be wrong by the Moon’s curvature, which drops 66 km below the tangent plane over the 480 km from PDI. Converting back to a position is a pair of rotations of the site’s unit vector by \(s/R\) and \(c/R\).

Inside the 3D scenes

Floating origin. A GPU stores positions as 32-bit floats, good to about one part in 16 million. Placed at Earth’s centre, the spacecraft would sit 400,000 km out and jitter by tens of metres. So each frame the scene is rebuilt around the spacecraft: the vehicle sits at the origin and Earth, the Moon and the terrain are translated by minus its position. Large objects keep their geometry relative to their own centres, and only the offsets change.

Depth. One scene spans 5 cm (the camera’s near plane) to 20 million km (the stars). A logarithmic depth buffer spreads depth precision evenly across that range so the Moon’s limb does not flicker through the spacecraft.

Earth is a latitude–longitude sphere textured from the same Natural Earth outlines as the 2D globe, flattened by 1/298.3 and turned about \(z\) by \(\text{GMST}_0 + \omega_\oplus t\). The Moon is placed by the body-to-working matrix above. The pages carry that matrix every hour or every 15 minutes, and linear interpolation of a rotation matrix is not quite a rotation, so the code re-orthonormalises it each frame by Gram–Schmidt: normalise the \(x\) column, remove its component from \(z\), take \(y = z\times x\).

Attitude. Each spacecraft’s orientation is built from two directions and the cross product that completes them. In Part 2 the docked stack points along the burn’s LVLH direction during burns and along the ecliptic normal, rolling at 0.3°/s, during passive thermal control; the hand-over into a burn is a quaternion slerp over 15 minutes with a smoothstep weight, so the stack turns instead of snapping. In Part 3 Eagle’s thrust axis is the reconstruction’s own thrust vector, exported from the simulation, and its windows are found by projecting “up” onto the plane perpendicular to that axis. The yaw to windows-up at 102:36:57 rotates that window direction 180° about the thrust axis over the documented 62 seconds.

Terrain. Part 3’s lunar surface is a polar mesh centred on the landing site: 230 rings spaced geometrically from 0.6 m to 950 km, and 360 spokes. Near the LM a cell is decimetres across; at the horizon, kilometres. The mesh is built in the Moon’s body frame relative to the site point, so its numbers stay small and precise, and it is placed each frame by the body matrix. Heights are sums of crater profiles; West crater and a handful of large named craters are at their approximate positions, and the rest are illustrative.

Cameras. “Chase” cameras use local vertical for up and the horizontal part of the velocity for “behind”, so the view stays level with the horizon. The “towards the Moon/Earth” views narrow the field of view to keep the body’s angular size readable and move the camera back so the spacecraft spans about a third of the frame. “Aldrin’s window” sits at the right-hand window in the LM’s own axes and looks forward and down about 45°, roughly where the 16 mm camera pointed.

Keeping it all in step

The pages never integrate physics. All of that happens offline in Python, and the result is one JSON file per part. The browser only interpolates and draws, which is what makes the synchronisation simple:

  1. Engine. Python and numpy compute every state in the working frame, keyed by GET and tagged documented, interpolated, propagated or fitted.
  2. Data file. One partN.json holds the track table, Moon and Sun tables, events and checks: the same time base and the same frame for every panel.
  3. One clock. A single reactive value \(T\) (Observable’s viewof T) is moved by the slider, the event chips or the player loop.
  4. Every view. Each panel interpolates the track at \(T\) and redraws; the 3D scene, the computer display, the transcript and the media are updated in the same pass.

The player. A requestAnimationFrame loop advances \(T\) by the elapsed wall-clock time times a rate. The rate is a warp schedule chosen per phase (240× in the coast before DOI, 10× through the braking phase, 2× in the last minutes) times the speed menu, or exactly 1 in Real time. The frame step is capped at 0.1 s so a hidden tab does not leap ahead when it comes back.

Reading the state at \(T\). A binary search on the GET column finds the row \(i\) with \(\text{GET}_i \le T < \text{GET}_{i+1}\); every column is then interpolated linearly with \(f = (T - \text{GET}_i)/(\text{GET}_{i+1} - \text{GET}_i)\), unwrapping longitudes across ±180° first. Rows are dense where things change quickly: 1 s in the last minutes of the landing, 2 s in the braking phase, 40 s in lunar orbit, 5 minutes in mid-coast. The few quantities derived in the browser use the same interpolation: Part 2’s osculating perilune and apolune (velocity from a centred difference over ±5 s, then the eccentricity vector), the revolution count (from line-of-sight crossings found in the track), and Part 3’s landing-point-designator angle.

Media. Every audio or film clip is mapped to mission time by segments, each a straight line between clip time and GET:

\[ t_\text{clip} = t_\text{clip start} + \frac{T - \text{GET}_\text{start}}{\text{rate}}. \]

The audio reels have rate 1 and re-seek if they drift more than 0.8 s. The 16 mm landing film has rate 5, because it was shot at 6 frames per second and transferred at 30; it plays at a fifth speed and re-seeks beyond 0.4 s. Audio plays only when the player runs at exactly real time; at any other speed the panel says where the next recording starts. When paused or scrubbing, the film seeks to the matching frame so it can be stepped with the slider. The transcript highlights the last line at or before \(T\), found by the same binary search, and scrolls itself unless the reader has scrolled it in the last four seconds.

Rendering. The 2D panels are rebuilt as SVG in one cell that depends on \(T\). The 3D scene sets its matrices in update(T) and marks itself dirty; its own animation loop renders only when something has changed, so a paused page costs almost nothing.

Where the data came from, and what it decided

Most sources were used for what they say. Several also forced a design decision, exposed an error in the source, or showed something missing from the model.

Source What it provided What it decided or revealed
Orloff & Garber, Apollo by the Numbers Ascent, orbit, coast, lunar-orbit and descent tables Its frame (mean of date) became the working frame. Its two altitude references (Fischer ellipsoid for positions, a sphere for apogee and perigee) explained why insertion altitude exceeded apogee. Two typesetting slips were corrected: a longitude of −81.1517° that would put the rocket west of the pad (taken as −80.1517°), and a flight-path angle of 44.94° where every neighbouring value fits 49.94°.
Boeing D5-15560-6, via Grant Hutchison The TLI state to more digits, including geocentric radius Used as the anchor for everything after TLI, in preference to the rounded table altitude.
Apollo Flight Journal maneuver PADs Noun 81 burn vectors, ignition times, LOS/AOS predictions Defined the LVLH convention and supplied priors for every fitted burn. The LOI PAD’s “AOS if no burn” time became one of the sharpest checks: 76:05:30 predicted, 76:05:30.4 reconstructed.
TRW trajectory reconstruction (1970) Event times, delta-v, landing-site height −8,557 ft Set the site radius, 1735.48 km, that every descent altitude is measured from.
Mission Director’s report; Apollo 11 Mission Report Sphere-of-influence crossing, DOI orbit, throttle-down, P64 altitude, the 21,000 ft navigation error, the 1,100 ft range extension Turned the descent from a fit to two endpoints into a fit to events along the way.
JPL DE421 through skyfield Moon and Sun positions The Mac that runs the blog has numpy but not skyfield, so the ephemeris is computed once and committed as a CSV, and the whole engine is numpy-only.
IAU lunar rotation model; LRO landing-site coordinates Moon orientation; Tranquility Base at 0.67416° N, 23.47314° E Selenographic positions in the same frame as modern maps. The 1969 report’s coordinates differ by about 0.04° because they used a 1969 map; the offset between the two placed the planned target in the modern frame.
PAO commentary (transcript and audio) Distances, speeds and altitudes read to the press Used only as checks. They turned out to be heights above the surface, not distances from the centre, and switched from Earth to the Moon as reference partway out.
The documented coast states States at separation, docking and midcourse correction 2 The midcourse correction would not fit until a missing 19.7 ft/s “evasive” burn at 4:40 was found in the PADs; Part 1 was corrected as a result.
Lunar-orbit states, 80–100 h Apolune and perilune over a day in orbit Showed the drift the model could not follow, the mascons. That is why Part 2 re-anchors at MCC-2 and Part 3 works backwards from PDI instead of continuing Part 2’s propagation.
Bennett, TM X-58040; Klumpp, R-695 Descent design, throttle limits, propellant budget; the guidance equations and the P66 control law Made it possible to simulate the descent with the LM’s own logic instead of an invented curve.
Luminary 99 pad loads (Virtual AGC) Descent targets; the LM’s mass, 33,664 lb at DOI Supplied the guidance targets directly. One target, the braking phase’s downrange velocity, is illegible in the scan, so it is one of the three fitted values.
Spacelog transcripts Words, speakers, timestamps, CapCom shifts; the descent callouts Callouts became the data for the last two minutes. The onboard recorder’s timestamps run a few seconds from the air-to-ground ones, so the air-to-ground time was used wherever a call appears in both.
NASA audio reels and the 16 mm film (Internet Archive) Sound and pictures The reels are edited, so each was pinned to GET by matching its speech-recognition captions to the transcript, segment by segment. The film runs five times fast; it was pinned at touchdown and checked against the documented yaw.

What the data could not settle

The descent engine’s efficiency is assumed (specific impulse 303 s), which sets the propellant margin. The braking-phase velocity target, the PDI position along the orbit and its flight-path angle are fitted, not documented. The crater field and maria outlines in the views are illustrative. And the model’s lunar gravity has only two terms, so it cannot reproduce the orbit changes the mascons caused; the posts say so wherever it shows.
