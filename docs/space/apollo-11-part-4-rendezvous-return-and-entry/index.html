Apollo 11, Part 4: Rendezvous, Return and Entry

Eagle leaves the Moon and catches Columbia, the burn behind the Moon that heads for home, two and a half days of coasting, and a fifteen-minute fall into the Pacific

A reconstruction of the last three days of Apollo 11, from lunar liftoff to splashdown. The ascent, the coelliptic rendezvous, transearth injection and the coast home are rebuilt from NASA’s tables and the crew’s readouts, and the entry is flown through a standard atmosphere with the bank angle fitted to the load-factor history.

This is the last of four posts that rebuild the flight of Apollo 11 from the numbers NASA published in 1969 and 1970.

  1. Launch to translunar injection: the ascent, the parking orbit, and the burn that leaves Earth.
  2. Translunar coast and lunar orbit: three days of coasting, one midcourse correction, and the burns that put Columbia and Eagle into lunar orbit.
  3. Descent and landing: Eagle undocks, drops to 50,000 feet, and flies twelve and a half minutes of powered descent to Tranquility Base.
  4. Rendezvous, return and entry (this post): ascent, rendezvous with Columbia, the burn home, and splashdown.

Part 3 ended with Eagle on the surface. Twenty-one and a half hours later, at 124:22:01, its ascent engine fired and the top half of the lander left the bottom half behind. This part covers the 71 hours from there to the Pacific: seven minutes of powered ascent, three and a half hours of rendezvous, Columbia’s burn out of lunar orbit, the coast home, and the entry.

How this part is built

The method is the same as before: documented anchors, physics between them, and a small number of quantities solved for so that the physics passes through the anchors. Everything else NASA published is a check. What is new here is that three of the four phases are steered, by guidance or by the crew, so the reconstruction has to fly them, not just coast between them.

  • The ascent is flown with a constant-thrust engine and a linear-tangent steering law, the textbook optimal law for climbing to orbit over a flat, airless planet. Its two constants, Eagle’s mass and the engine’s exhaust velocity are fitted to the insertion state Aldrin read off his display.
  • The rendezvous is flown burn by burn: each of the first two burns is solved so that the orbit after it matches the one NASA’s tables give, and the third, the one that sets up the intercept, is solved so that Eagle reaches Columbia.
  • Columbia’s orbit is fitted to the times Mission Control gave for losing and regaining its signal behind the Moon, and to the heights and speeds in the tables.
  • Transearth injection is fitted to arrive at the documented entry point, and the entry is flown through the US Standard Atmosphere, with the bank angle solved so that the deceleration matches what the command module’s accelerometers recorded.

tabAt = (rows, t, n) => {
  const ts = rows._t ?? (rows._t = rows.map(d => d[0]));
  const i = Math.max(0, Math.min(rows.length - 2, d3.bisectRight(ts, t) - 1));
  const a = rows[i], b = rows[i + 1], f = Math.max(0, Math.min(1, (t - a[0]) / ((b[0] - a[0]) || 1)));
  return d3.range(1, n + 1).map(j => a[j] + f * (b[j] - a[j]));
}

Liftoff

Eagle’s ascent stage carried one engine, with no throttle and no gimbal: 3,500 lbf, on or off. Everything about the ascent came from pointing it. For ten seconds after liftoff the computer held Eagle vertical, to clear the descent stage and the ground, then pitched it over sharply, and for the next seven minutes tipped it steadily further towards the horizontal until it was in orbit.

The pitch programme the reconstruction uses is the linear-tangent law,

\[ \tan\theta(t) = a - b\,t, \]

where \(\theta\) is the thrust’s elevation above the local horizontal. Over a flat surface with constant gravity and no air, this is the law that reaches a given height and speed with the least propellant, which is why it, or something very close, sits inside almost every ascent guidance scheme from Apollo onwards. The Moon’s surface is curved and its gravity points to its centre, so it is not exactly optimal here, but over 160 n mi of travel the difference is small.

Two steering constants, the liftoff mass and the engine’s exhaust velocity are solved for. The four conditions they have to meet are the height, climb rate and speed at engine cutoff, which Aldrin read off the display at 124:29:21 (“5537.3, 32.8 feet per second, 60,666”), and the ascent’s total delta-v from NASA’s tables. The fitted liftoff mass is 10,800 lb, and the fitted exhaust velocity corresponds to a specific impulse of 307 s, close to the ascent engine’s rating; the propellant this burns is within 1% of Bennett’s 4,934 lb. None of that was imposed.

The orbit Eagle reached, about 48 by 9 n mi, matches the tables and the onboard estimates. So does one number nobody planned for. Twenty-nine minutes after insertion Collins told Eagle its out-of-plane rate was minus 1 ft/s. The reconstruction’s Eagle lifts off from Tranquility Base, which by then had been carried almost two miles out of Columbia’s orbital plane by the Moon’s rotation, and it flies straight down the plane’s direction without steering sideways. It arrives with an out-of-plane rate of −0.95 ft/s, and the crew decided not to correct it.

Fitting the ascent turned up something about NASA’s tables. For the rendezvous rows that does not work. The rendezvous

Columbia was 60 n mi up and about 250 n mi ahead of Eagle along the orbit. Eagle did not chase it. A spacecraft that speeds up climbs into a higher, slower orbit and falls behind; to catch something ahead of you in orbit, you go lower, not faster. Eagle’s 9 by 48 n mi orbit had a shorter period than Columbia’s, and it gained on Columbia by about 9° per hour just by being lower.

Apollo 11 used the coelliptic rendezvous, designed for Gemini and flown on every lunar landing until Apollo 14. It has three burns, each with a single purpose:

  1. CSI, concentric sequence initiation, half an orbit after insertion at the high point of Eagle’s orbit, raises the low point so that Eagle’s orbit is nearly circular.
  2. CDH, constant delta height, half an orbit later, makes Eagle’s orbit coelliptic with Columbia’s: the same shape, a constant 15 n mi below. Seen from Columbia, Eagle now slides forward along a straight line underneath it.
  3. TPI, terminal phase initiation, starts the intercept when the line of sight to Columbia reaches a set elevation above the horizon. From there Eagle travels 130° round the Moon to meet Columbia, with two small midcourse corrections and then braking by hand.

The reason for the indirection is that each burn is small and each error is easy to see and fix, and the final approach always looks the same to the crew: Columbia rising against the stars at a fixed angle, the same every time, which they had flown hundreds of times in the simulator.

Relative motion

The left panel’s rendezvous view is the classic picture: Columbia fixed at the origin, Eagle plotted as height and distance relative to it. In that frame the motion follows the Clohessy–Wiltshire equations. For a target in a circular orbit with mean motion \(n\), small offsets \(x\) (ahead) and \(z\) (above) obey

\[ \ddot x = -2n\dot z, \qquad \ddot z = 3n^2 z + 2n\dot x. \]

A spacecraft a constant height \(\Delta h\) below drifts forward at \(\dot x = \tfrac{3}{2} n\,\Delta h\); with \(\Delta h = 15\) n mi and Columbia’s two-hour orbit that is about 1.2 n mi a minute, so the 60 n mi between them after CDH closed to the 27 n mi of TPI in 46 minutes. The loops before CSI are Eagle’s eccentric orbit seen from a nearly circular one.

The burns

CSI and CDH are each solved so that the orbit after the burn is the one NASA’s tables give. The crew read their burn solutions to the ground, and those are checks:

The CDH solution agrees with the one the crew read out to within 2 ft/s in each component, and the height difference it leaves, 15.8 n mi, is close to the 15.5 n mi Eagle’s backup guidance computer gave. TPI is solved differently, as the burn that puts Eagle on Columbia at the end of the braking, 42 minutes later, and it comes out at 20.3 ft/s against the 25.3 flown. Its components depend on exactly where Columbia was along its orbit, which no table gives; this is the place where the reconstruction is least constrained, and the next section explains how Columbia’s orbit is pinned down.

Braking and docking

TPI puts Eagle on a collision course; the closing speed has to come off by hand. From 127:37 Armstrong braked in steps, keeping the line-of-sight rate to Columbia small and the closing rate in proportion to the range, a gate of so many feet per second at each mile. The reconstruction’s path here follows the range and rate calls in the transcript, along the line of sight of the intercept:

checks("rdv", c => c[1].startsWith("Range at"))

Without braking, the intercept would have arrived with a closing speed of about 30 ft/s. At 127:52 Eagle was stationkeeping about 100 ft from Columbia, and Collins flew the last few feet. The docking was the one rough moment of the rendezvous: as the latches closed, the two spacecraft yawed against each other by 15° or so, “a funny one”, Collins said afterwards, before the latches caught. Armstrong and Aldrin came back through the tunnel with the rock boxes. At 130:09 Eagle was let go, and half an hour later Columbia moved 2.2 ft/s away from it.

Transearth injection

At 135:23:42, behind the Moon on its 31st orbit, Columbia’s service propulsion engine fired for two and a half minutes and added 3,279 ft/s. It turned a 54 by 63 n mi orbit into a hyperbola relative to the Moon, and a long, falling ellipse relative to Earth that met the atmosphere 59½ hours later.

The energy of the escape is set by the burn’s size: \(v_\infty^2 = v^2 - 2\mu_\text{Moon}/r\), a speed at infinity of about 4,000 ft/s relative to the Moon. Its direction is set by where around the orbit the burn happens, and its timing and aim at Earth by both. The reconstruction keeps the documented ignition time and takes the burn’s direction from the pre-burn PAD (3,201.1 ft/s forward, 681.8 sideways, 265.0 up), then solves for small corrections to its pitch and yaw and for its size so that, with the one midcourse correction actually flown, the trajectory crosses 400,000 ft at the documented time, latitude, longitude and angle. The sideways component in the PAD is real: the trajectory home had to leave the Moon’s equatorial orbit at an angle to reach the right latitude on Earth.

Entry

At 195:03:06 the command module, separated from its service module a quarter of an hour earlier, reached 400,000 ft, 3° south of the equator, 1,500 n mi from its target, moving at 36,194 ft/s and descending at 6.5°. The angle is the whole problem of lunar entry. Steeper than about 7.5° and the deceleration exceeds what the crew and structure were built for; shallower than about 5.5° and the command module does not lose enough speed on its first pass to stay in, and skips back out on a long ellipse. The corridor is two degrees wide at the end of a trajectory 240,000 miles long, and MCC-5’s 4.8 ft/s was what kept it centred.

The command module was not a ballistic capsule. Its centre of mass was offset from its axis, so it flew at an angle of attack of about 20° and produced lift, about 0.28 of its drag. By rolling, the computer pointed that lift up, down, or sideways, and so controlled how deep the command module flew and how far. The reconstruction flies it as a point mass with drag \(D = \tfrac12\rho v^2 C_D A\) and lift \(L = 0.282\,D\), in the US Standard Atmosphere 1976 turning with Earth, with the 12,148 lb weight the onboard computer used:

\[ \dot{\mathbf v} = \mathbf g(\mathbf r) - \frac{D}{m}\,\hat{\mathbf v}_\text{rel} + \frac{L}{m}\left(\cos\sigma\,\hat{\mathbf u}_\perp + \sin\sigma\,\hat{\mathbf v}_\text{rel}\times\hat{\mathbf u}_\perp\right), \]

where \(\sigma\) is the bank angle and \(\hat{\mathbf u}_\perp\) is the local vertical with its component along the velocity removed. The bank angle is held at zero (lift up) for the first 70 seconds and rolled to the −15° the display showed, as the 1970 entry analysis describes. After that it is described by its cosine, the share of lift pointing up, at eleven times, plus the time of the first reversal of its sideways component; the later reversals are at the times the analysis gives. These are solved so that the speed at the start of the final phase, the load factor at the bottom of the loft, the second load-factor peak, the time of drogue deployment and the splash point match the record.

Splashdown

The drogue parachutes opened at about 24,000 ft, 9 minutes after entry interface, and the three mains at about 10,000 ft. The reconstruction treats both as drag only, with no wind (there was a surface wind of about 18 knots), and solves for the two ballistic coefficients that put the mains out at 10,000 ft and the command module in the water at the mission report’s 195:18:35.

checks("splash")

The main-parachute descent comes out at 32 ft/s at the water, which is what three 83-foot canopies were designed to give. Once the transcript’s clock is aligned to the mission report’s splashdown time, Armstrong’s last three altitude calls on the way down agree with the reconstruction to within about 250 ft; the first, 3,750 ft, is 840 ft below it, which suggests the drogue phase was a little slower than this simple drag model. Columbia came down upside down, in the “stable 2” position, and righted itself when the flotation bags inflated. The Hornet’s swimmers were there within ten minutes; the crew, in biological isolation garments, were on the carrier an hour after splashdown, 8 days, 3 hours and 18 minutes after leaving Florida.

What the reconstruction shows

Across the four parts, the reconstruction has flown Apollo 11 from the launch pad to the Pacific through 195 hours, re-anchoring at each phase but otherwise letting physics carry it. A few points hold across the whole flight:

  • NASA’s 1969–70 numbers are self-consistent to a degree that is easy to take for granted. Burn sizes, times, states and orbits from different documents, produced by different groups, agree with each other through Newtonian physics to a few feet per second, often less.
  • The weakest link is the Moon’s gravity. The model’s lunar field has its two largest terms. Over a few hours that is good to a mile or two; over a day it is not, and every lunar part needed a fresh anchor for where along the orbit the spacecraft were.
  • The record that pins things best is often the least formal one. Signal-loss times read out by Mission Control, Aldrin’s altitude and speed calls, the Public Affairs Officer’s range figure before TPI, and the crew’s readbacks of their burn solutions are the checks that constrain this part most tightly.
  • Where the reconstruction does not fit, it says so. The descent used 2.6% more delta-v than Eagle did; the TPI burn differs in its components; the entry’s bank history is one of several that would do. Those are the places the 1969 record does not fully determine.

Method and sources

For how the coordinate frames, views, synchronisation and data sources fit together across the series, see the explainer Frames of Reference in the Apollo 11 Reconstruction.

The force model is the one used throughout: Earth with \(J_2\), the Moon with \(J_2\) and \(C_{22}\), the Sun, integrated with a Dormand–Prince 5(4) method in the geocentric mean-of-1969 frame, with the Moon and Sun from JPL’s DE421. The entry uses Earth with \(J_2\) and the atmosphere only, integrated with fourth-order Runge–Kutta at half-second steps.

The pipeline:

  1. Columbia’s state at 131:00 (position along the orbit, height, speed, flight-path angle) is fitted to eight signal-loss and acquisition times, the tables’ heights and speeds at docking, jettison, final separation and TEI, the orbit after final separation, the crew’s TPI readback, and the PAO’s range and range rate before TPI. The plane comes from Part 3’s orbit carried forward.
  2. The ascent: two steering constants, the liftoff mass and the exhaust velocity, fitted to the DSKY insertion state, the tables’ speed and the ascent delta-v. Thrust 3,500 lbf (Bennett), 10 s vertical rise.
  3. CSI and CDH: forward and radial components fitted to the documented orbits after each (CDH’s sideways component from the crew’s readback); finite burns on the LM’s RCS. TPI: three components solved for an intercept at the end of TPF. Braking follows the range calls.
  4. TEI: pitch, yaw and size, from the PAD direction, fitted to the entry-interface time, place and angle, and to the TEI cutoff state; MCC-5 as flown (PAD vector, ABTN time).
  5. Entry: bank profile fitted as described above; drogues and mains as drag, two ballistic coefficients fitted.
