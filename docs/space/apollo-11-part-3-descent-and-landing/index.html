Apollo 11, Part 3: Descent and Landing

Undocking, the burn behind the Moon that dropped Eagle to 50,000 feet, and twelve and a half minutes of guided and hand-flown descent to Tranquility Base

A reconstruction of Apollo 11’s lunar descent from undocking to touchdown. The braking and approach phases are flown with the LM’s own guidance law and the Luminary 99 targets; the last two minutes follow the crew’s callouts. Covers descent orbit insertion, the 1202 alarms, the landing point designator, and Armstrong’s manual landing.

This is the third of four posts that rebuild the flight of Apollo 11 from the numbers NASA published in 1969 and 1970.

  1. Launch to translunar injection: the ascent, the parking orbit, and the burn that leaves Earth.
  2. Translunar coast and lunar orbit: three days of coasting, one midcourse correction, and the burns that put Columbia and Eagle into lunar orbit.
  3. Descent and landing (this post): Eagle undocks, drops to 50,000 feet, and flies twelve and a half minutes of powered descent to Tranquility Base.
  4. Rendezvous, return and entry: ascent, rendezvous with Columbia, the burn home, and splashdown.

Part 2 ended at 100 hours, with the two spacecraft still docked in a 63 by 56 nautical mile orbit. This part covers the next three hours: undocking, the separation of Columbia and Eagle, the burn behind the Moon that lowered Eagle’s orbit, and the powered descent itself. The descent is the most heavily documented part of the flight, and also the part where the 1969 tables are thinnest, because almost all of it happened under closed-loop guidance. So this part rebuilds the guidance as well as the trajectory.

How this part is built

As before, the reconstruction works from anchors: documented states, burn times and results. What is new is the powered descent, which is reconstructed in three pieces.

  • The braking phase is simulated with the LM’s own guidance law. The computer in Eagle steered with a quadratic guidance law, described by its designer Allan Klumpp, aimed at a target state stored in its memory. The targets for Apollo 11 are in the published listing of the Luminary 99 software’s pad loads. The reconstruction flies that law, with the descent engine’s documented throttle schedule, from the documented PDI state.
  • The approach phase uses the same law, with the approach targets. It is checked against Aldrin’s altitude callouts and against the angles he read off the landing point designator.
  • The last two minutes are fitted to the callouts. Armstrong took over at about 400 feet. From there the path is flown by a controller like the LM’s rate-of-descent mode, steered to follow the altitudes, descent rates and forward speeds Aldrin called out, and to end at the landing site at contact light.

A handful of quantities that no surviving table gives are solved for, and the post says which ones as it goes. Everything else is a check.

tabAt = (rows, t, n) => {
  const ts = rows._t ?? (rows._t = rows.map(d => d[0]));
  const i = Math.max(0, Math.min(rows.length - 2, d3.bisectRight(ts, t) - 1));
  const a = rows[i], b = rows[i + 1], f = Math.max(0, Math.min(1, (t - a[0]) / (b[0] - a[0])));
  return d3.range(1, n + 1).map(j => a[j] + f * (b[j] - a[j]));
}

Undocking and separation

At 100 hours 12 minutes, behind the Moon, Eagle undocked. When the signal came back six minutes later Armstrong reported “The Eagle has wings.” For the next twenty minutes the two spacecraft flew in formation while Armstrong turned Eagle round in front of Columbia’s window so that Collins could look over its landing gear. Then, at 100:39:53, Collins fired Columbia’s thrusters for 9 seconds, a gentle push of 2.7 ft/s straight down, towards the Moon.

A push towards the Moon does not make a spacecraft drop away. A radial burn changes the shape of the orbit but not its size, so the period is the same as before. Relative to Eagle, Columbia traces a closed loop once per revolution, about 0.5 n mi below and then above, and up to 2 n mi behind, and returns to where it started an orbit later. It is a small, safe separation that keeps the two spacecraft in sight of each other without either one having to plan around the other, which is why it was chosen.

The reconstruction does not start at undocking. It starts at PDI, an hour later, and is traced backwards through the descent orbit insertion burn to here. These checks, then, test the whole chain:

checks("orbit")

The separation burn and the orbit after it match NASA’s tables to within about a mile. The undocking state is further out, 7.5 n mi in height and 33 ft/s in speed. The tables give only height and speed, not position along the orbit, and an orbit 63 by 56 n mi passes through 62.9 n mi twice each revolution. The 1969 orbit’s low point had been dragged around by the mascons (Part 2), and this reconstruction’s lunar gravity, with only its two largest terms, does not follow it. Half an hour later, at the separation burn, the reconstruction and the tables agree again.

Descent orbit insertion

On the next pass behind the Moon, at 101:36:14, Eagle’s descent engine fired for 30 seconds, at 10% thrust and then 40%, and slowed it by 76.4 ft/s. That is less than 1.5% of its orbital speed, but it was enough to drop the far side of its orbit from about 60 nautical miles to about 8. Half an orbit later, at its lowest point, Eagle would be 50,000 feet above the surface, about 260 miles east of the landing site.

This is the same trick as the transfer orbits in Parts 1 and 2, in miniature: a small burn on one side of an orbit moves the opposite side a long way. To lower perilune by \(\Delta r\) from a near-circular orbit of radius \(r\) at speed \(v\), the burn needed is about

\[ \Delta v \approx \frac{v}{4}\,\frac{\Delta r}{r} = \frac{5{,}340\ \text{ft/s}}{4} \times \frac{52\ \text{n mi}}{1{,}000\ \text{n mi}} \approx 70\ \text{ft/s}. \]

checks("doi")

The height of the low point is the number that mattered, and it is the one that is hardest to pin down. The tables give 57.8 n mi at DOI cutoff; the onboard computer worked out an orbit of 57.2 by 9.1 n mi, against 8.5 predicted, all measured from the landing site’s radius. The reconstruction’s descent orbit, traced back from the documented PDI state, is 59.8 by 7.9 n mi. A mile at perilune was well within what the guidance could absorb: Bennett’s 1970 analysis notes that an overburn of only 12 ft/s, 3 seconds, would have put Eagle on a path that hit the Moon. Its two-stage throttle schedule was there to make the cutoff precise.

Powered descent initiation

At 102:33:05 Eagle’s descent engine lit again, at 10% thrust, to settle the propellant and let the engine’s gimbal centre on the centre of mass. Twenty-six seconds later the computer commanded full thrust. Eagle was 47,000 feet above the landing site, moving at 5,560 ft/s, and 264 nautical miles from where it would land.

Here the reconstruction does its main piece of solving. NASA’s tables give the time, height and speed at PDI. They do not give where along the orbit Eagle was, or the exact slope of its path. Those two numbers, together with one illegible guidance target, are found so that the simulated descent, flown by the LM’s own guidance law, reproduces three documented events: the throttle coming down 386 seconds after ignition, Eagle reaching 7,129 feet at 102:41:32, when the computer switched to the approach phase, and, traced backwards, the 57.8 n mi height at DOI cutoff.

checks("pdi")

The fitted flight-path angle is a quarter of a degree, close to the zero expected at the low point of the descent orbit, and the range is close to Bennett’s “about 260 n mi”. The 47,444 ft height is above the landing site; the crew’s estimate from timing landmarks was 52,000 ft, and the computer’s own figure differed from the landing radar’s by 2,900 ft when the radar locked on, the “DELTA-H” Aldrin called at 102:38:04.

The braking phase

Eagle’s computer did not follow a stored trajectory. Every two seconds it worked out an acceleration command from where it was and where it wanted to be. Klumpp’s guidance law makes the commanded acceleration a quadratic in the time-to-go \(T\) (negative, counting up to zero at the target):

\[ \mathbf a_\text{cmd} = \frac{12\,(\mathbf r_T - \mathbf r)}{T^2} + \frac{6\,(\mathbf v_T + \mathbf v)}{T} + \mathbf a_T, \]

in a frame centred on the landing site with \(x\) up, \(z\) downrange and \(y\) across. The targets \(\mathbf r_T, \mathbf v_T, \mathbf a_T\) are not the landing site; they are a point some way beyond the end of the phase, chosen on the ground so that the path arrives at “high gate” in the right attitude and at the right height for the crew to see the landing area. \(T\) itself comes from the downrange component, as the root of a cubic,

\[ j_{Tz} T^3 + 6\,a_{Tz} T^2 + (18\,v_{Tz} + 6\,v_z)\,T + 24\,(r_{Tz} - r_z) = 0, \]

which makes the time-to-go consistent with the target jerk \(j_{Tz}\). The descent engine provides the thrust: the guidance’s command minus gravity. Here the design meets the hardware. The descent engine could not be throttled between 65% and about 92% of its rated 10,500 lbf without damaging its injector, so for most of the braking phase it ran at a fixed 92.5%, and the guidance used only the direction of its command. As the vehicle lightened, the thrust it asked for fell, and when it fell below 57%, the computer took over the throttle. On Apollo 11 that “throttle recovery” came at 386 seconds, against 384 planned.

checks("p63")

Simulated with the Luminary 99 targets, the braking phase passes through high gate at 7,131 ft, with time-to-go to its target point 56 s (the targets were set about 60 s beyond high gate), and arrives 133 ft/s down where the mission report gives 125.

The alarms

Four minutes into the burn, as Eagle yawed to put its windows up and its landing radar towards the surface, the crew turned to the computer display and saw a program alarm: 1202. It recurred at 102:39:02, and three more, 1201 and 1202, came in the approach phase. Both codes meant that the computer had more work queued than it had time to do and had restarted, dropping the lower-priority jobs and keeping the guidance running. The cause, found after the flight, was the rendezvous radar’s interface sending a stream of spurious counts that took up roughly an eighth of the computer’s time. The guidance never stopped. Steve Bales in Mission Control, with Jack Garman’s list of alarm codes, called “GO” on each one; the reconstruction marks them on the display in the last panel.

The approach phase

At 102:41:32 the computer switched to the approach program, P64, with a new set of targets close to the landing site, and pitched Eagle nearly upright, so that for the first time the crew could see where they were going. The display now showed a landing point designator angle: Armstrong could look through a scale etched on his window, at that angle, and see where the computer was taking him. Aldrin read the angle out three times. The reconstruction can compute it from the geometry, as the angle of the line of sight to the computer’s aim point below Eagle’s horizontal plane:

checks("lpd")

These are independent checks, not inputs, and they agree to within 2°. They also confirm the geometry of what happened next. The point the guidance was flying to was not the planned landing site. A small error in Eagle’s velocity at PDI, from undocking and venting that the navigation had not counted, had left the computer about 21,000 ft uprange of where it thought it was, and it was steering for a point about four miles beyond the planned site. The crew had noticed early, from landmarks passing “a little long”. What the designator showed Armstrong was a crater the size of a football stadium, West crater, with a field of boulders around it.

Armstrong flies the last two minutes

At about 102:43:20, 410 feet up and moving forward at 50 to 60 ft/s, Armstrong switched to the rate-of-descent mode, P66. The computer still controlled the throttle, holding whatever descent rate Armstrong set with a switch; he flew the attitude, and so the horizontal speed, by hand. He kept going, over West crater and its boulder field, looking for a clear place.

That last part of the path is reconstructed from what Aldrin called out, nearly every few seconds: height, descent rate, and forward speed. The model is flown by a controller with the same structure as P66, a 1.5-second time constant on the descent rate and thrust kept within 20° of vertical, that follows those calls and ends at the landing site at contact light.

The calls fit together well, with one exception. Added up, the forward speeds Aldrin read cover only about 2,600 ft, while the automatic approach left Eagle about 3,500 ft from where it landed when Armstrong took over. To close the gap, the reconstruction’s forward speed starts at the automatic speed and comes down to the calls over about a minute; it runs 5 to 15 ft/s above them through P66. The calls were the computer’s estimate of velocity, updated by the landing radar, and the timing of a spoken number is uncertain by a second or two, so a difference of this size is within what the record can resolve. The altitudes and descent rates are matched to about a nominal callout’s precision once Armstrong is flying, and the larger altitude differences come in P64, where the automatic path runs 5 seconds behind the calls.

The purple dashed line in the charts and the side view is where the computer would have taken Eagle without him. It reaches its target, 111 ft above the aim point, about 55 seconds before the actual contact light, 1,100 ft short of where Eagle landed, at the western edge of West crater and its boulders.

Sixty seconds

On the ground, the descent engine’s propellant was watched second by second. When a low-level sensor in the tanks tripped, a clock started: when it ran out, the crew would have 20 seconds to land or abort. Charlie Duke called “60 seconds” at 102:45:02 and “30 seconds” at 102:45:31. Contact light came at 102:45:40.

checks("total")

The reconstruction’s descent uses 6,949 ft/s, 173 ft/s (2.6%) more than the 6,775.6 ft/s NASA’s tables give, and lands with 650 lb of usable propellant, about 73 seconds of hover. Bennett’s post-flight analysis puts the margin at touchdown at 40 to 50 seconds. Both differences point the same way: the reconstruction flies a little less efficiently than Eagle did, most of it in the braking phase, where the fitted path is about 4,900 ft further from the site and 75 ft/s faster at high gate than Bennett’s nominal. The propellant figures also depend on the engine’s efficiency, here taken as a specific impulse of 303 s; an engine about 2% less efficient, 296 s, would bring the margin down to Bennett’s figure. The low-level warning had in fact come early: propellant sloshing in the tanks uncovered the sensor, and post-flight analysis found Eagle had more left than the crew were told.

“Houston, Tranquility Base here. The Eagle has landed.” Eagle had come down about four miles from the planned target, which is what a 21,000 ft error at PDI would do, plus Armstrong’s 1,100 ft. The Sun was 10.6° above the eastern horizon in the reconstruction, low enough for every boulder to throw a long shadow, which was why the landing time was set when it was. Collins, in Columbia, was 64 n mi up and about 230 n mi to the west, already moving on round the Moon. On the passes that followed he searched for Eagle through his sextant and never found it.

Method and sources

For how the coordinate frames, views, synchronisation and data sources fit together across the series, see the explainer Frames of Reference in the Apollo 11 Reconstruction.

The trajectory before PDI is the force model of Parts 1 and 2: the Moon with its \(J_2\) and \(C_{22}\) terms, Earth and the Sun as third bodies, integrated with a Dormand–Prince 5(4) method. For the descent, the LM is simulated with the guidance computed every 2 seconds and held between updates, as the computer did, with a 2.2 s lead time; the engine is a constant-thrust rocket with an exhaust velocity of 2.97 km/s, and the vehicle’s mass starts from the 33,664 lb in the Luminary 99 pad load at DOI, less the propellant DOI used. Everything is computed in the Moon-centred, mean-of-1969 frame, with the landing site turning with the Moon.

The pipeline:

  1. The LM’s orbital plane is Columbia’s, from Part 2’s reconstruction. The guidance’s aim point is placed 1,100 ft uprange and 400 ft across from where Eagle landed (mission report, Bennett).
  2. The PDI state takes the documented time, height and speed. Its position along the orbit, its flight-path angle and the braking-phase downrange velocity target (unreadable in the scanned pad load) are solved for by least squares against the throttle-down time, the height at P64, and the DOI cutoff height.
  3. The braking and approach phases are flown by Klumpp’s guidance law with the Luminary 99 targets.
  4. From 102:43:22 the path follows the crew’s callouts with a P66-style controller, ending at the landing site.
  5. The coast is traced back from PDI through DOI (76.4 ft/s) to undocking, and Columbia is given its separation burn.

The other figures in the post, among them the separation orbit, the DOI states, the LPD angles, the callouts, the descent delta-v and the propellant margin, were not used in the fit.
