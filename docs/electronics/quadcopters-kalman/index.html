How the smartphone made the drone, how a fixed-pitch rotor makes lift, how four of them steer in three dimensions, and how filtering noisy gyroscopes and accelerometers keeps an unstable machine in the air

A quadcopter is the simplest aircraft there is to build and among the hardest to fly. It has four motors, four propellers fixed to their shafts, no hinges, no swashplate and no control surfaces: nothing moves except the motors. Everything a helicopter does with an intricate rotor head, a quadcopter does by changing the speeds of four fans. The price of that mechanical simplicity is that the machine is unstable. Left to itself it tips over in a fraction of a second, so it needs to measure its own attitude and correct it hundreds of times a second. Fifteen years ago the sensors and computers to do that were aerospace parts. Today they cost less than the propellers.

This post covers how that happened, the physics of a fixed-pitch rotor, how four of them combine to move in three dimensions, and how a flight controller turns two kinds of poor sensor into one good estimate of attitude, with complementary and Kalman filters. There is a quadcopter to fly, a hover calculator, and simulations of the estimators and of a flight controller using them.

How the cheap quadcopter happened

The idea is old. Louis and Jacques Breguet’s Gyroplane No. 1 lifted its pilot about 60 centimetres in September 1907 on four rotors, steadied by four men on the ground. George de Bothezat’s “Flying Octopus” hovered for the US Army in 1922, and Étienne Oehmichen’s quadrotor set the first rotorcraft distance records in 1924. The Convertawings Model A of 1956 flew forward, controlled purely by varying the thrust of its four rotors. The configuration was abandoned for the single main rotor with a tail rotor, partly because varying the thrust of four rotors by hand, fast enough to balance the machine, is beyond a pilot. The modern quadcopter is that old configuration with the pilot’s job handed to electronics, and it became cheap when a handful of technologies, most of them paid for by the smartphone, arrived together between about 2008 and 2013. Chris Anderson, who co-founded the drone maker 3D Robotics in 2009, called it “the peace dividend of the smartphone wars”.

MEMS inertial sensors. A quadcopter must measure its rotation rate and the direction of gravity continuously. Micro-electromechanical (MEMS) gyroscopes and accelerometers, vibrating structures etched in silicon, were pushed into mass production by games controllers and phones: the Wii MotionPlus in 2009, the iPhone 4’s gyroscope in 2010. InvenSense, whose gyroscope was inside the MotionPlus, announced the first single chip with a three-axis gyroscope and a three-axis accelerometer, the MPU-6000 family, in November 2010, and the average price of a sensor kept falling as phone volumes grew. MultiWii, one of the first open flight-controller projects, began around 2010 by reading the gyroscope out of a Wii MotionPlus.

Cheap fast microcontrollers and open firmware. Fusing the sensors and running control loops at a kilohertz needs modest but real computing. First 8-bit Arduino-class chips and then 32-bit ARM Cortex-M microcontrollers did it for a dollar or two. Open-source projects did the control engineering in public: ArduPilot, from the DIY Drones community, added multirotors in 2010; PX4 grew out of Lorenz Meier’s work at ETH Zurich from 2008; and the MultiWii, Baseflight, Cleanflight and Betaflight line (Betaflight from 2015) became the firmware of racing.

Brushless motors and fast speed controllers. A quadcopter steers only by changing rotor speed, so its motors must change thrust within milliseconds. Brushless outrunner motors from radio-controlled aircraft gave the power-to-weight; electronic speed controllers running community firmware (SimonK from 2011, then BLHeli) tuned for response rather than smoothness gave the speed. Later came a digital motor protocol, DShot, in 2016, and in 2019 the use of each motor’s measured speed to filter the gyroscope.

Lithium-polymer batteries. Hovering is expensive, as the next section shows. LiPo cells, made in vast numbers for consumer electronics, combined high energy density with discharge rates that rose from two or three times their capacity per hour around 2002 to twenty times by 2004, and higher since.

Radios, cameras and satellite navigation. Spread-spectrum 2.4 GHz control links replaced crystal-tuned ones; phone camera modules and cheap video transmitters made first-person flying possible; inexpensive GNSS receivers, barometers and magnetometers gave position hold and return-to-home, which turned a pilot’s toy into a camera platform anyone could fly. Gimbals that hold the camera steady with brushless motors, driven by the same MEMS sensors (DJI’s Zenmuse in 2012, open-source controllers from 2013), gave smooth aerial video.

The Shenzhen supply chain. Under all of it sat high-volume manufacturing in southern China. Parrot’s smartphone-controlled AR.Drone, unveiled in January 2010, showed there was a consumer market; DJI, founded in Shenzhen by Frank Wang in 2006, made aerial photography a product with the Phantom in January 2013; by 2014 toy quadcopters cost less than a good book.

The common thread is the trade the quadcopter makes: no mechanical complexity, in exchange for sensing and computation fast enough to keep an unstable machine in the air. The rest of this post is about that trade.

How a fixed rotor makes lift

A propeller pushes air down; by Newton’s third law the air pushes the propeller up. The simplest quantitative model, momentum theory, treats the rotor as an actuator disc of area \(A\) that adds momentum to the air passing through it, ignoring the blades entirely. In hover, air arrives from rest far above, passes the disc at the induced velocity \(v_i\), and leaves far below at \(2v_i\) (the contraction of the slipstream is what doubles it). The mass flow through the disc is \(\dot m = \rho A v_i\), and the thrust is the rate at which momentum leaves:

\[ T = \dot m \cdot 2 v_i = 2\rho A v_i^2, \qquad\text{so}\qquad v_i = \sqrt{\frac{T}{2\rho A}}. \]

The power that goes into the air is thrust times the velocity at the disc:

\[ P_{\text{ideal}} = T v_i = \frac{T^{3/2}}{\sqrt{2\rho A}}. \]

Two facts follow. Power grows as thrust to the power 3/2, so heavier craft are disproportionately expensive to keep in the air. And for a given thrust, power falls as the square root of disc area: large slow rotors are efficient, small fast ones are not. The ratio \(T/A\), the disc loading, is the single number that sets hover efficiency; a large helicopter has a disc loading of a few hundred newtons per square metre, a small quadcopter around a hundred, a hummingbird far less. Real rotors lose more to blade drag, tip losses and swirl, which is summarised by the figure of merit \(\text{FM} = P_{\text{ideal}}/P_{\text{shaft}}\), around 0.5 to 0.65 for small rotors, the best measured micro-air-vehicle rotors reaching about 0.65.

How thrust depends on speed comes from dimensional analysis. A rotor of diameter \(D\) turning at \(n\) revolutions per second moves air at speeds proportional to \(nD\) over an area proportional to \(D^2\), so

\[ T = C_T\, \rho\, n^2 D^4, \qquad Q = C_Q\, \rho\, n^2 D^5, \]

where \(Q\) is the torque needed to turn it and the coefficients \(C_T\) and \(C_Q\) depend on blade shape but hardly on speed. Thrust and torque both go as the square of rotor speed. That is the only fact about propellers the flight controller needs, and the quadratic is why a quadcopter commands squared speeds, \(u_i = \omega_i^2\), rather than speeds.

Four rotors, six directions

A rigid body in space has six degrees of freedom: three of position and three of orientation. A quadcopter has four inputs, the four rotor speeds. It cannot control six things independently with four, so it is underactuated, and the way round that is the essence of how it flies.

Number the rotors front-left, front-right, rear-right, rear-left in the usual X arrangement, each at distance \(\ell\) from the centre, so at \(\pm a = \pm \ell/\sqrt 2\) along each axis. Use axes with \(x\) forward, \(y\) to the left and \(z\) up. Each rotor makes a thrust \(f_i = k_T \omega_i^2\) along the body’s \(z\) axis and a reaction torque \(k_Q\omega_i^2\) about it, opposite to its spin. Diagonal rotors spin the same way: front-left and rear-right clockwise seen from above, the other two anticlockwise. Adding up forces and moments about the centre gives four quantities:

\[ \begin{pmatrix} T \\ \tau_x \\ \tau_y \\ \tau_z \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 & 1 \\ a & -a & -a & a \\ -a & -a & a & a \\ \kappa & -\kappa & \kappa & -\kappa \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} f_{\text{FL}} \\ f_{\text{FR}} \\ f_{\text{RR}} \\ f_{\text{RL}} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \kappa = k_Q / k_T . \]

Read the rows one at a time:

  • Total thrust \(T\) is the sum of all four. Speed all four up together and the craft climbs.
  • Roll torque \(\tau_x\): the left pair against the right pair. Speed up the left rotors and slow the right ones by the same amount, and the total thrust is unchanged but the craft rolls to the right.
  • Pitch torque \(\tau_y\): the rear pair against the front pair. More thrust at the back pitches the nose down.
  • Yaw torque \(\tau_z\): the clockwise pair against the anticlockwise pair. Each rotor drags the body round the opposite way to its spin; with the pairs balanced, the drags cancel, which is why diagonal rotors spin the same way. Speed up the clockwise pair and slow the anticlockwise pair, and the body turns anticlockwise, with no change in thrust.

The matrix, called the mixer, is invertible, so any combination of thrust and three torques can be produced by solving for the four rotor thrusts. Its rows are orthogonal, so the inverse is just its transpose with each row scaled: each rotor’s command is a quarter of the thrust plus or minus a share of each torque. That is the entire “mixing” step in every flight controller.

What the mixer cannot do is push sideways. Every rotor’s thrust points along the body’s \(z\) axis, so horizontal motion has to come from tilting the whole craft: roll a few degrees to the right and part of the thrust now points right. With the craft tilted by small angles \(\phi\) (roll) and \(\theta\) (pitch) and the thrust balancing weight, the horizontal accelerations are

\[ \ddot x \approx g\,\theta, \qquad \ddot y \approx -g\,\phi, \qquad \ddot z = \frac{T\cos\phi\cos\theta}{m} - g . \]

Four inputs give six motions by chaining them: a torque changes the angular rate, the rate changes the tilt, the tilt changes the horizontal acceleration, which changes velocity and position. From rotor speed to sideways position is four integrations. The rotational part obeys Euler’s equations, \(J\dot{\boldsymbol\omega} + \boldsymbol\omega \times J\boldsymbol\omega = \boldsymbol\tau\), which for gentle flight reduce to \(J\dot\omega = \tau\) on each axis.

Why a quadcopter is unstable

Hovering is an equilibrium, but not a stable one. Take a single axis and linearise: the roll angle \(\phi\) obeys \(J\ddot\phi = \tau\), with almost no aerodynamic restoring moment. The transfer function from torque to angle is \(1/(Js^2)\), a double integrator with both poles at the origin. Any disturbance, a gust, a motor slightly weaker than the others, a propeller with a nick in it, produces a torque that integrates into an ever-growing angle. And the angle feeds the horizontal acceleration, so sideways drift is a quadruple integrator, \(g/(Js^4)\).

Feedback fixes this. Measure the angular rate and the angle and feed them back, \(\tau = -K_p(\phi - \phi_{\text{des}}) - K_d\dot\phi\), and the closed loop becomes \(J\ddot\phi + K_d\dot\phi + K_p\phi = K_p\phi_{\text{des}}\): a damped oscillator that settles to the commanded angle. The faster the loop and the better the measurement of \(\phi\) and \(\dot\phi\), the stiffer it can be made. Real flight controllers use a cascade of such loops, each running faster than the one outside it:

Loop Measures Commands Typical rate
Rate (innermost) Angular rate, from the gyroscope directly Torques, via the mixer 1–8 kHz
Attitude Roll and pitch angle, estimated Desired angular rates 250 Hz – 1 kHz
Velocity and position Velocity and position, estimated from GNSS, barometer, optical flow and the inertial sensors Desired tilt angles and thrust 10–100 Hz

The innermost loop is easy to feed: a gyroscope measures angular rate directly. The loops outside it need quantities no single sensor measures well. Attitude is the first of them, and getting it right is a problem in statistics.

Two bad sensors make one good one

A flight controller has two sensors for attitude, and both are flawed in complementary ways.

The gyroscope measures angular rate \(\omega\). Integrate it and you get the change in angle. But every MEMS gyroscope has a bias \(b\), a small offset that drifts with temperature, plus noise:

\[ \omega_{\text{meas}} = \omega + b + n_\omega . \]

Integrating turns the bias into an angle error that grows linearly, \(b\,t\), and the noise into a random walk that grows as \(\sqrt t\). A bias of 3°/s, unremarkable for a cheap part warming up, is 30° of error after ten seconds. The gyroscope is accurate over short times and useless over long ones.

The accelerometer measures specific force: acceleration minus gravity, in the body’s axes. At rest it measures gravity alone, and the direction of gravity gives the tilt directly: \(\phi \approx \operatorname{atan2}(f_y, f_z)\). It has no drift. But it is noisy, because the motors shake the frame at hundreds of hertz, and any real acceleration of the craft corrupts it. There is a subtler problem that took the field some years to state clearly. On a multirotor in steady flight, the rotors’ thrust is along the body \(z\) axis, so in the body’s \(x\) and \(y\) directions the accelerometer measures only aerodynamic drag, not gravity: an ideal accelerometer on an ideal quadcopter, tilted and accelerating sideways, would read straight down the body axis and report no tilt at all. In practice it works on average, because the rotors’ drag couples velocity into the reading and accelerations average out, but it means the accelerometer is trustworthy over long times and misleading over short ones.

One sensor is good at high frequencies, the other at low frequencies. The obvious move is to take each where it is good.

The complementary filter

Integrate the gyroscope, and continually nudge the result a small fraction of the way towards the accelerometer’s angle:

\[ \hat\phi_k = \alpha\left(\hat\phi_{k-1} + \omega_{\text{meas}}\,\Delta t\right) + (1-\alpha)\,\phi_{\text{acc},k}, \qquad 0 < \alpha < 1 . \]

In the frequency domain, with time constant \(\tau = \alpha\Delta t/(1-\alpha)\), this is

\[ \hat\phi(s) = \underbrace{\frac{\tau s}{1 + \tau s}}_{\text{high-pass}} \frac{\omega_{\text{meas}}(s)}{s} + \underbrace{\frac{1}{1 + \tau s}}_{\text{low-pass}} \phi_{\text{acc}}(s): \]

the gyroscope’s angle through a high-pass filter, the accelerometer’s through a low-pass filter, with the two filters summing to exactly one so the true angle passes through undistorted. With \(\alpha = 0.995\) at 500 Hz the crossover is about 0.4 seconds: the gyroscope is trusted for faster changes, the accelerometer for slower ones. It is a few lines of code, and some form of it flies most hobby quadcopters: Betaflight, for example, estimates attitude with Robert Mahony’s nonlinear complementary filter on the full 3D rotation, not a Kalman filter. Its weakness is that \(\alpha\) is a guess, and that a constant gyro bias leaves a constant angle error, \(b\,\tau\).

The Kalman filter

Rudolf Kálmán’s filter, published in 1960 and extended by Stanley Schmidt’s group at NASA Ames into the form used for Apollo’s navigation, answers the question the complementary filter leaves open: given models of how the state evolves and how noisy each sensor is, what blend is best? It treats the unknowns as random variables, keeps a mean and a covariance for them, and updates both each time step in two moves.

For attitude, make the gyroscope bias part of the state, so the filter can learn it:

\[ \mathbf x = \begin{pmatrix} \phi \\ b \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf x_k = \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -\Delta t \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}}_{F} \mathbf x_{k-1} + \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} \Delta t \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}}_{B} \omega_{\text{meas},k} + \mathbf w_k, \qquad \phi_{\text{acc},k} = \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}}_{H} \mathbf x_k + v_k . \]

The process noise \(\mathbf w \sim \mathcal N(0, \mathsf Q)\) says how much the angle and bias can wander beyond what the model predicts; the measurement noise \(v \sim \mathcal N(0, \mathsf R)\) says how noisy the accelerometer’s angle is.

Predict. Push the estimate and its uncertainty through the model:

\[ \hat{\mathbf x}_{k|k-1} = F\hat{\mathbf x}_{k-1} + B\,\omega_{\text{meas},k}, \qquad P_{k|k-1} = F P_{k-1} F^\top + \mathsf Q . \]

Update. Compare the prediction with the accelerometer, and correct by an amount weighted by the relative uncertainties:

\[ K_k = P_{k|k-1}H^\top \left(H P_{k|k-1} H^\top + \mathsf R\right)^{-1}, \qquad \hat{\mathbf x}_k = \hat{\mathbf x}_{k|k-1} + K_k\left(\phi_{\text{acc},k} - H\hat{\mathbf x}_{k|k-1}\right), \qquad P_k = (I - K_k H) P_{k|k-1} . \]

The Kalman gain \(K\) is the heart of it. When the prediction is uncertain relative to the measurement, \(K\) is large and the filter believes the accelerometer; when the measurement is noisy relative to the prediction, \(K\) is small and it believes the gyroscope. For linear models with Gaussian noise this is the minimum-mean-square-error estimator, and the best linear one whatever the noise distribution. Two things make it better than the complementary filter here. Its second state learns the gyroscope bias from the persistent disagreement between the integrated gyro and the accelerometer, and subtracts it. And its gain is derived from the noise levels rather than guessed. In fact, run long enough with fixed noise levels, \(K\) settles to a constant and the angle update becomes exactly a complementary filter, with the crossover set optimally by the ratio of the noise variances and with an integral term for the bias: the complementary filter is the steady-state Kalman filter for a particular choice of \(\mathsf Q\) and \(\mathsf R\).

The Kalman filter learns the bias within a few seconds (the lower chart) and then tracks both fast and slow motion. Raise the bias to see the complementary filter’s offset grow while the Kalman filter’s does not; set Kalman’s assumed accelerometer noise far from the real value to see that the filter is only as good as its noise model: too small and it chases the accelerometer’s noise, too large and it reacts slowly.

In three dimensions

Real attitude is a rotation in three dimensions, not one angle, and rotations do not add like vectors. The filters above generalise in two ways. Mahony’s and Madgwick’s filters apply the complementary idea directly on the rotation group: the cross product between the measured and predicted gravity directions gives a correction rate, fed back with proportional and integral gains, the integral being the bias estimate. An extended Kalman filter linearises the nonlinear rotation dynamics about the current estimate at each step and runs the equations above on the linearisation, usually with the attitude error kept as a small rotation vector so that the quaternion stays normalised (the multiplicative or error-state formulation). PX4’s EKF2 (24 states in its 2020 form: orientation, velocity, position, gyroscope and accelerometer biases, the Earth’s and the body’s magnetic fields, and wind) and ArduPilot’s EKF3 estimate everything in one filter, fusing the inertial sensors with GNSS, barometer, magnetometer, optical flow and rangefinders, each with its own measurement model and noise.

The Kalman filter’s real strength shows there, in fusing many sensors with different rates, delays and failure modes: a GNSS fix every 200 ms, a barometer that drifts with the weather, a magnetometer disturbed by motor currents, an optical-flow camera that works only near the ground. Each new measurement is just another update with its own \(H\) and \(\mathsf R\), arriving whenever it arrives.

With the Kalman filter, or with the complementary filter, the flight is almost indistinguishable from flying with perfect knowledge of the angle. With the gyroscope alone the attitude estimate drifts; the controller holds the estimated angle level, so the real craft tilts by the accumulated error and accelerates away, and the position loop cannot save it once the hidden tilt is large. With the accelerometer alone it flies, but the noise goes straight into the attitude loop and the craft twitches through every manoeuvre: the tilt trace is ragged, and on a real machine that noise would heat the motors and blur the camera.

Two practical points sit outside these simple models. First, the rate loop uses the gyroscope directly and runs fastest, so its enemy is not bias but vibration: propellers and motors shake the frame at their rotation frequencies and harmonics, hundreds of hertz, aliasing into the measurement. Modern firmware filters the gyroscope with low-pass and notch filters whose centre frequencies follow the measured speed of each motor, which is why speed feedback from the motor controllers mattered so much. Second, the accelerometer’s weakness in accelerated flight is why serious autopilots fuse GNSS velocity and model the rotor drag explicitly rather than trusting tilt from the accelerometer.

Method and sources

Simulations. All three run in your browser from quadsim.js and quad3d.js. The hover calculator applies momentum theory with the stated figure of merit, 80% electrical efficiency, air density 1.225 kg/m³ and \(C_T = 0.1\) for the speed. The 3D quadcopter is a rigid body (mass 0.6 kg, inertia 0.004, 0.004, 0.007 kg m², arm 0.11 m, torque-to-thrust ratio 0.016 m) integrated at about 1 kHz, with an angle controller on roll and pitch, a rate controller on yaw and light aerodynamic damping; with stabilisation off the sticks set torques directly. The attitude simulation runs at 500 Hz for 20 s: the true angle follows a second-order response to a sequence of targets; the gyroscope adds a bias (with a slow random walk) and white noise of 1.5°/s; the accelerometer angle adds white noise and an error proportional to angular acceleration standing in for linear acceleration. The Kalman filter uses the two-state model above with process noise of 0.5°/√s on the angle and 0.05 °/s/√s on the bias. The planar simulation flies a 0.6 kg craft through four waypoints with a cascaded PD controller, rotor drag of 0.25 N s/m, and the same sensor models; it stops if the craft hits the ground, tilts past 80° or leaves the area.
