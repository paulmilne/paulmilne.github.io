Embeddings, Part 1: From Words to Vectors

How a language model turns text into numbers: tokenisation, the embedding table, word2vec and what it really factorises, analogies, and how position is added

The first layer of every large language model is a table of vectors, one per token. This post builds a byte-pair tokeniser, derives word2vec’s objective and shows it factorises shifted pointwise mutual information, tests the famous analogies, explains how a transformer learns its own embeddings, and derives sinusoidal and rotary position encodings. Real vectors throughout: word2vec trained on text8, GloVe, and GPT-2.

A language model never sees a letter. Before any of the machinery people talk about, attention, layers, billions of parameters, the text is cut into pieces and each piece is replaced by a list of numbers: its embedding. Everything the model knows about a word has to be reachable from that list, or built up from it.

This is the first of two posts on embeddings. This one is about the vectors inside the model: how text is cut into tokens, how the table of token vectors is learned, what the geometry of those vectors means and does not mean, and how a model is told where in the sentence each token sits. Part 2 is about the separate embedding models that turn a whole sentence or document into one vector, and how they are used to search and to feed language models the right documents.

Nothing here is illustrative. The tokeniser is trained on text8, the first 100 million characters of an English Wikipedia dump; word2vec is trained on the same text; the analogy tests use the 6-billion-token GloVe vectors; and the transformer section uses GPT-2’s own embedding table.

The embedding table

Fix a vocabulary of \(V\) tokens and a dimension \(d\). The embedding is a matrix \(E \in \mathbb R^{V \times d}\), one row per token. Token \(t\) becomes row \(t\):

\[ \mathbf e_t = E^{\top}\mathbf x_t, \]

where \(\mathbf x_t \in \{0,1\}^V\) is the one-hot vector with a single 1 in position \(t\). The matrix product is how it is written; in practice it is an array lookup. The point of writing it as a product is that the lookup is linear, so the gradient of anything with respect to \(E\) is easy to find, which is how the table gets learned.

The table is large. GPT-2’s smallest model has \(V = 50{,}257\) tokens and \(d = 768\), so \(E\) holds 38.6 million numbers: almost a third of that model’s 124 million parameters. Recent models have bigger vocabularies and wider vectors: Llama 3 uses 128,256 tokens of dimension 4,096 in its 8-billion-parameter version, over half a billion numbers for the input table alone.

Two choices sit behind that table: what the \(V\) tokens are, and how the rows get their values. The next sections take them in turn.

Cutting text into tokens

The obvious unit is the word, and it fails twice. English has an unbounded supply of words (names, numbers, misspellings, “tokenisation”), so any fixed list leaves some out, and those become an unknown-word symbol the model can learn nothing about. And a list long enough to cover most text would make \(E\) enormous, with most rows seen too rarely to learn. Characters fix both problems and create a third: sequences become five or six times longer, and the cost of attention grows with the square of the sequence length.

Subword tokenisation sits between them. Frequent words get one token; rare words are spelled out from common pieces. The method used by the GPT family is byte-pair encoding (BPE). Philip Gage published it in 1994 as a data compression algorithm: repeatedly find the most frequent pair of adjacent bytes and replace it with a new byte that does not occur in the data. Rico Sennrich, Barry Haddow and Alexandra Birch adapted it for machine translation in 2016. As a tokeniser it is learned once, from a corpus:

  1. Start with every word spelled as characters, plus an end-of-word marker (written _ here).
  2. Count every adjacent pair of symbols, weighted by how often each word occurs.
  3. Merge the most frequent pair into a single new symbol, everywhere. Record the merge.
  4. Repeat until the vocabulary is the size you want.

To tokenise new text, apply the recorded merges in the order they were learned. I ran this on text8: 17,005,207 words, 253,854 of them distinct.

bpe = FileAttachment("data/bpe8k.json").json()
rank = { const m = new Map(); bpe.merges.forEach(([a, b], i) => m.set(a + "\u0000" + b, i)); return m; }
bpeWord = (w, n) => {
  let s = [...w, "_"];
  for (;;) {
    let best = -1, bi = -1;
    for (let i = 0; i < s.length - 1; i++) {
      const r = rank.get(s[i] + "\u0000" + s[i + 1]);
      if (r !== undefined && r < n && (best < 0 || r < best)) { best = r; bi = i; }
    }
    if (bi < 0) return s;
    s = [...s.slice(0, bi), s[bi] + s[bi + 1], ...s.slice(bi + 2)];
  }
}

The first merges are the most common pairs in English: e + _ (a word ending in e), s_, th, in, er, then th + e_ to make the word the by the tenth merge. By a hundred merges the common short words are whole tokens: for, was, that, with, and the number words that text8 spells out. By a thousand, frequent suffixes such as ation and ings have their own tokens. The cost of the text falls fast and then slowly:

Sixteen thousand merges bring the average to 1.16 tokens a word, against 5.88 symbols a word with characters alone. That is the trade the vocabulary size controls.

GPT-2 made two changes. It works on bytes rather than characters, so the 256 possible bytes are the starting alphabet and no input can ever be unrepresentable: an emoji or a Chinese character is just a few bytes. And before merging, the text is split by a regular expression into words, numbers and punctuation, with a leading space kept attached to the word that follows it. GPT-2’s 50,257 tokens are the 256 bytes, 50,000 merges and one end-of-text marker. Its vocabulary therefore has " token" (with the space) and "token" (without) as different tokens with different embeddings. Here is GPT-2’s tokeniser next to the one used by GPT-4o, which has about four times the vocabulary:

The spaces are shown as ␣. Numbers are a well-known weak point: GPT-2 cuts digit strings wherever its merges happen to fall, so the same number can be split differently in different contexts, which is one reason early models were poor at arithmetic. Later tokenisers split numbers into fixed groups of digits.

Other subword schemes work on the same principle with a different rule for choosing what to merge or keep. WordPiece, used by BERT, merges the pair that most increases the likelihood of the training data under a unigram model; the Unigram method in SentencePiece starts from a large vocabulary and prunes it. All of them produce the same kind of object: a fixed list of \(V\) strings, each to be given a vector.

Meaning from company

Where do the vectors come from? The idea that makes it possible is old. Zellig Harris proposed in 1954 that words occurring in similar contexts tend to have similar meanings, the distributional hypothesis; J. R. Firth put it as “You shall know a word by the company it keeps” in 1957. If that is right, meaning can be measured by counting.

Count, over a corpus, how often word \(w\) appears within a few positions of context word \(c\): call it \(\#(w,c)\), with \(\#(w) = \sum_c \#(w,c)\), \(\#(c)\) likewise and \(|D|\) the total number of pairs. Raw counts are dominated by frequent words (everything co-occurs with the), so the useful measure is how much more often the pair occurs than chance:

\[ \operatorname{PMI}(w,c) = \log \frac{P(w,c)}{P(w)\,P(c)} = \log\frac{\#(w,c)\,|D|}{\#(w)\,\#(c)}. \]

This is pointwise mutual information. A word’s row of PMI values against every context is already a vector that captures meaning: coffee and tea have high PMI with cup, drink and hot. But the rows are as long as the vocabulary and mostly empty (unobserved pairs have PMI \(-\infty\), usually clipped to zero). The classical fix is to compress the matrix: take the singular value decomposition \(M = U\Sigma V^\top\) and keep the top \(d\) singular values. By the Eckart–Young theorem, \(U_d\Sigma_d V_d^\top\) is the best rank-\(d\) approximation to \(M\) in the Frobenius norm, and the rows of \(U_d\Sigma_d^{1/2}\) are \(d\)-dimensional word vectors. Applied to word–document counts, this is latent semantic analysis (Deerwester and colleagues, 1990).

word2vec

In 2013 Tomas Mikolov and colleagues at Google published word2vec, which learned word vectors with a shallow neural network instead of by counting and factorising. It was fast, it scaled to billions of words, and its vectors did something that made it famous: the analogies of the next section. Its most successful form is the skip-gram with negative sampling (SGNS).

Every word \(w\) gets two vectors: \(\mathbf w\) when it is the centre word and \(\mathbf c\) when it is a context word. Slide a window along the text. Each (centre, context) pair seen in the window is a positive example, and the model should say “yes, these occur together” with probability \(\sigma(\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf c)\), where \(\sigma(x) = 1/(1+e^{-x})\). For each positive pair, draw \(k\) negative context words at random from a noise distribution \(P_n\); for those the model should say “no”. The loss for one pair is

\[ \ell(w,c) = -\log\sigma(\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf c) \;-\; \sum_{i=1}^{k}\log\sigma(-\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf c_i), \qquad c_i \sim P_n. \]

Using \(\sigma'(x) = \sigma(x)\,(1-\sigma(x))\), so that \(\frac{d}{dx}\log\sigma(x) = 1-\sigma(x) = \sigma(-x)\), the gradients are

\[ \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \mathbf w} = -\sigma(-\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf c)\,\mathbf c + \sum_{i=1}^k \sigma(\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf c_i)\,\mathbf c_i, \qquad \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \mathbf c} = -\sigma(-\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf c)\,\mathbf w, \qquad \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \mathbf c_i} = \sigma(\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf c_i)\,\mathbf w. \]

Read as an update, gradient descent pulls \(\mathbf w\) towards the true context by an amount proportional to how wrong the model was (\(\sigma(-\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf c)\) is the probability it gave to “no”), and pushes it away from each noise word in proportion to how much it wrongly believed in it. Only \(k+2\) vectors change per pair, which is what made it cheap. The original paper uses \(k\) between 5 and 20, a noise distribution of unigram frequencies raised to the power \(\tfrac34\) (which samples rare words more often than their frequency would), and randomly discards very frequent words so that the and of do not dominate the training pairs.

What word2vec actually learns

For a few years word2vec looked like a different kind of thing from counting and SVD. Omer Levy and Yoav Goldberg showed in 2014 that it is not. Collect the loss over the whole corpus and group the terms by the pair \((w,c)\). The pair appears as a positive \(\#(w,c)\) times, and as a negative \(k\,\#(w)\,P_n(c)\) times on average. Writing \(x = \mathbf w\cdot\mathbf c\), the part of the total loss that depends on \(x\) is

\[ L(x) = -\#(w,c)\,\log\sigma(x) \;-\; k\,\#(w)\,P_n(c)\,\log\sigma(-x). \]

Suppose the dimension is large enough that each dot product can be chosen freely. Setting \(L'(x) = 0\):

\[ -\#(w,c)\,\sigma(-x) + k\,\#(w)\,P_n(c)\,\sigma(x) = 0 \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; e^{x} = \frac{\sigma(x)}{\sigma(-x)} = \frac{\#(w,c)}{k\,\#(w)\,P_n(c)}, \]

so the optimum is

\[ \mathbf w\cdot\mathbf c = \log\frac{\#(w,c)}{\#(w)\,P_n(c)} - \log k = \operatorname{PMI}(w,c) - \log k, \]

with \(P_n\) standing in for \(P(c)\). Skip-gram with negative sampling is factorising the matrix of pointwise mutual information, shifted down by \(\log k\), as \(W C^\top\). It is a weighted, implicit version of the counting method it replaced. The difference in practice comes from the weighting, the smoothed noise distribution and the subsampling, not from a different principle.

The theorem assumes unlimited dimension and full convergence. To see how close a real model comes, I trained word2vec with gensim on all of text8 (100 dimensions, window 5, \(k = 5\), 5 epochs; 71,290 words kept) and counted the co-occurrences of the 3,000 most frequent words with the same window weighting and subsampling the training used. For the 247,000 pairs seen at least 20 times:

GloVe (Jeffrey Pennington, Richard Socher and Christopher Manning, 2014) makes the factorisation explicit. It fits the log of the co-occurrence counts \(X_{ij}\) directly, by weighted least squares:

\[ J = \sum_{i,j} f(X_{ij})\left(\mathbf w_i\cdot\tilde{\mathbf w}_j + b_i + \tilde b_j - \log X_{ij}\right)^2, \qquad f(x) = \min\!\left(1, (x/x_{\max})^{3/4}\right), \]

with biases \(b_i, \tilde b_j\) absorbing the \(\log \#(w)\) and \(\log \#(c)\) terms of PMI, and \(f\) stopping rare, noisy pairs from dominating and very common ones from swamping everything. The published GloVe vectors trained on 6 billion tokens of Wikipedia and newswire are used below.

The geometry of meaning

Similarity between two embeddings is measured by the cosine of the angle between them, \(\cos\theta = \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v / (\|\mathbf u\|\,\|\mathbf v\|)\). Length mostly reflects how often a word occurs and how consistently it is used; direction carries the meaning. Here are the nearest neighbours in the GloVe space (100 dimensions, the 20,000 most frequent words, stored here to 8 bits):

glove = {
  const g = await FileAttachment("data/glove20k.json").json();
  const bin = Uint8Array.from(atob(g.q), c => c.charCodeAt(0));
  const q = new Int8Array(bin.buffer), N = g.words.length, D = g.dim;
  const U = new Float32Array(N * D);
  for (let i = 0; i < N; i++) {
    let s = 0;
    for (let j = 0; j < D; j++) { const v = q[i * D + j] * g.scale[i] / 127; U[i * D + j] = v; s += v * v; }
    s = Math.sqrt(s); for (let j = 0; j < D; j++) U[i * D + j] /= s;
  }
  const idx = new Map(g.words.map((w, i) => [w, i]));
  return {U, N, D, words: g.words, idx};
}
gvec = (w) => { const i = glove.idx.get(w); return i === undefined ? null : glove.U.subarray(i * glove.D, (i + 1) * glove.D); }
nearest = (v, k, exclude = []) => {
  const {U, N, D, words} = glove, ex = new Set(exclude), out = [];
  let n = 0; for (let j = 0; j < D; j++) n += v[j] * v[j]; n = Math.sqrt(n);
  for (let i = 0; i < N; i++) {
    let s = 0; for (let j = 0; j < D; j++) s += U[i * D + j] * v[j];
    out.push([s / n, i]);
  }
  out.sort((a, b) => b[0] - a[0]);
  return out.filter(([, i]) => !ex.has(words[i])).slice(0, k).map(([s, i]) => ({word: words[i], cos: s}));
}
viewof nnWord = Inputs.text({value: "physics", label: "Word", submit: "Find"})
nnOut = {
  const v = gvec(nnWord.trim().toLowerCase());
  if (!v) return htl.html`<p class="em-note">Not among the 20,000 most frequent words.</p>`;
  const r = nearest(v, 11).slice(1);
  return htl.html`<table class="table table-sm em-t" style="max-width:360px"><tbody>${r.map(d => htl.html`<tr><td>${d.word}</td><td class="num">${fmt(d.cos, 3)}</td></tr>`)}</tbody></table>`;
}

Try bank, whose neighbours are all financial: a single vector per word has to average over every sense of it, and the commonest sense wins. That is the main limitation of static embeddings, and the reason transformers exist, as the next section explains.

Analogies, and a caveat

The result that made word2vec famous was that relationships appear as directions. If king is to man as queen is to woman, then \(\mathbf{king} - \mathbf{man} + \mathbf{woman}\) should land near \(\mathbf{queen}\). The test set Mikolov’s group released has 19,544 such questions in fourteen categories, from capital cities to plural verbs. The method, called 3CosAdd, answers \(a : b :: c : ?\) with

\[ \hat d = \arg\max_{x}\;\cos\!\left(\mathbf x,\; \mathbf b - \mathbf a + \mathbf c\right), \qquad x \notin \{a, b, c\}. \]

The last condition matters more than its size suggests. For GloVe on the 19,230 questions whose words are all in its top 100,000:

Allow the input words as answers and accuracy falls from 64% to 34%. Of the 12,717 answers then wrong, 10,392 are \(c\) itself, the word the offset \(\mathbf b - \mathbf a\) was added to, and another 1,308 are \(b\). The nearest vector to \(\mathbf{king} - \mathbf{man} + \mathbf{woman}\) in GloVe is king itself, at a cosine of 0.84; queen is second, at 0.77. Offsets between related words are small compared with the distance between unrelated ones, so the sum stays close to its starting point, and the test only works because the starting point is ruled out. Tal Linzen (2016) and Malvina Nissim and colleagues (2020) made this point at length. The parallelogram is real, the categories where it works best (capitals, nationalities, comparatives) are real, but the famous example is weaker than its reputation.

The same geometry carries the corpus’s biases. Tolga Bolukbasi and colleagues showed in 2016 that \(\mathbf{programmer} - \mathbf{man} + \mathbf{woman}\) in a widely used word2vec model landed near homemaker. Embeddings record how words are used, not how they should be.

Inside a transformer

A transformer language model does not train its embeddings separately. The table \(E\) is just the first layer, initialised at random and learned with everything else, from one task: predict the next token.

Write \(\mathbf h \in \mathbb R^d\) for the model’s final hidden state at some position, after all the layers have mixed in context from earlier tokens. The output layer scores every token in the vocabulary with a matrix \(W \in \mathbb R^{V\times d}\), \(\mathbf z = W\mathbf h\), and turns the scores into probabilities with the softmax, \(p_v = e^{z_v}/\sum_u e^{z_u}\). If the true next token is \(y\), the loss is the cross-entropy \(L = -\log p_y\). Its gradient with respect to the scores is the standard result

\[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial z_v} = p_v - [v = y], \]

so for the output matrix,

\[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf w_v} = (p_v - [v=y])\,\mathbf h. \]

The row for the correct token is pulled towards \(\mathbf h\); every other row is pushed away from it, in proportion to the probability the model wrongly gave it. Compare the input table: its gradient reaches only the rows of tokens that actually appeared in the input. The two tables are trained very differently.

Many models, GPT-2 among them, use the same matrix for both, \(W = E\). Ofir Press and Lior Wolf (2017) and Hakan Inan and colleagues (2017) showed this tying works as well or better while saving \(V d\) parameters, and it has a clean reading: the model predicts the next token by producing a hidden state that points in the direction of that token’s embedding. Tying also has a side effect. Every update pushes all the rows that were not the answer away from \(\mathbf h\), and rare tokens are almost never the answer, so they receive nothing but these pushes. Jun Gao and colleagues (2019) showed this drives rare token embeddings into a narrow cone, in a common direction, the “representation degeneration problem”. GPT-2’s table, below, shows it.

The embedding is only the starting point. The vector for bank is the same whether the sentence is about rivers or money; the attention layers that follow combine it with the vectors around it, and by the middle layers the representation of bank in “river bank” and in “bank account” have moved apart. Kawin Ethayarajh (2019) measured this, and also found that these contextual vectors occupy a narrow cone of the space, the same anisotropy as the static table. Part 2 is about what to do with those contextual vectors.

Where am I in the sentence?

Attention, the operation at the heart of a transformer, compares every token’s vector with every other token’s and mixes them according to the comparison. It has no notion of order. If \(X\) is the matrix of input vectors and \(P\) any permutation matrix, self-attention satisfies \(\operatorname{Attn}(PX) = P\operatorname{Attn}(X)\): shuffle the words and the outputs are shuffled in the same way and otherwise unchanged. “Dog bites man” and “man bites dog” would be the same input. (A decoder’s causal mask, which lets each token see only earlier ones, breaks the symmetry slightly, and models can infer some position from it; but every major model adds position explicitly.) Position has to be added.

Learned positions. The simplest answer is a second table, one vector per position, added to the token embedding. GPT-2 does this: a \(1{,}024 \times 768\) table, learned like everything else.

Sinusoidal positions. Ashish Vaswani and colleagues, in the 2017 paper that introduced the transformer, added a fixed vector to each token instead:

\[ \mathrm{PE}(p, 2i) = \sin\!\left(\frac{p}{10000^{2i/d}}\right), \qquad \mathrm{PE}(p, 2i+1) = \cos\!\left(\frac{p}{10000^{2i/d}}\right). \]

Each pair of coordinates is a point going round a circle at frequency \(\omega_i = 10000^{-2i/d}\), from one radian per position down to one cycle in about 63,000 positions. The reason for sines and cosines is that a shift is linear. By the angle-addition formulas,

\[ \begin{pmatrix}\sin\omega(p+k)\\ \cos\omega(p+k)\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}\cos\omega k & \sin\omega k\\ -\sin\omega k & \cos\omega k\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}\sin\omega p\\ \cos\omega p\end{pmatrix}, \]

so \(\mathrm{PE}(p+k)\) is a fixed rotation of \(\mathrm{PE}(p)\), the same for every \(p\). A layer can learn to attend “three tokens back” with one linear map.

Rotary positions. Most current models use a refinement, rotary position embedding, or RoPE (Jianlin Su and colleagues, 2021). Instead of adding a position vector to the input, it rotates the query and key vectors inside each attention layer. Split a \(d\)-dimensional vector into \(d/2\) pairs and treat each pair as a complex number; at position \(m\), multiply pair \(i\) by \(e^{\mathrm i m\theta_i}\), with \(\theta_i = 10000^{-2i/d}\) as before. Attention compares a query \(\mathbf q\) at position \(m\) with a key \(\mathbf k\) at position \(n\) through their dot product, which for one pair is the real part of \(q\,\bar k\):

\[ \operatorname{Re}\!\left[(q\,e^{\mathrm i m\theta})\,\overline{(k\,e^{\mathrm i n\theta})}\right] = \operatorname{Re}\!\left[q\,\bar k\,e^{\mathrm i (m-n)\theta}\right]. \]

The absolute positions cancel; only the offset \(m-n\) remains. Summing over pairs, the attention score depends on the content of the two tokens and on how far apart they are, and nothing else, without adding anything to the token’s embedding. RoPE also builds in a decay with distance. The panel takes a random query, sets the key equal to it, so that without rotation they would match perfectly, and plots their rotary score against the distance between them. Each pair contributes \(|q_i|^2\cos\big((m-n)\theta_i\big)\), so the score is a sum of cosines at \(d/2\) frequencies:

  d: Inputs.select([16, 64, 128], {value: 128, label: "Dimension d"}),
  base: Inputs.select([100, 10000, 500000], {value: 10000, label: "Base (θᵢ = base^(−2i/d))"}),
  seed: Inputs.range([1, 20], {value: 3, step: 1, label: "Random vector"})
})
ropePlot = {
  const {d, base, seed} = rope;
  let s = seed * 9301 + 49297; const rnd = () => { s = (s * 9301 + 49297) % 233280; return s / 233280; };
  const g = () => Math.sqrt(-2 * Math.log(rnd() + 1e-9)) * Math.cos(2 * Math.PI * rnd());
  const q = Array.from({length: d}, g);
  const nrm = q.reduce((a, x) => a + x * x, 0);
  const pts = [];
  for (let off = 0; off <= 2000; off += 2) {
    let sc = 0;
    for (let i = 0; i < d / 2; i++) {
      const th = Math.pow(base, -2 * i / d), r2 = q[2 * i] ** 2 + q[2 * i + 1] ** 2;
      sc += r2 * Math.cos(off * th);
    }
    pts.push({off, sc: sc / nrm});
  }
  return Plot.plot({width: Math.min(width, 680), height: 260, marginLeft: 50, grid: true,
    x: {label: "Distance between query and key (tokens)"}, y: {label: "Score relative to distance 0", domain: [-0.6, 1.02]},
    marks: [Plot.ruleY([0]), Plot.line(pts, {x: "off", y: "sc", stroke: "#1d3557", strokeWidth: 1.4})]});
}

The fast pairs fall out of phase within a few tokens, so the score drops quickly at first; the slow pairs, whose wavelengths run to tens of thousands of tokens, hardly turn at all over this range, and the score levels off at the share they carry. The base sets that balance: lower it to 100 and almost every pair turns fast, so the score falls to noise around zero; raise it and more of the score survives at long range. Llama 3 raised the base from 10,000 to 500,000 to stretch the useful range to longer documents, and later methods for extending context work mostly by rescaling these frequencies.

Terminology and where it comes from

Term Meaning here Origin
Token One unit of text in a model’s vocabulary Old English tācen, a sign or symbol. Charles Sanders Peirce’s distinction between a type (the word the) and its tokens (each occurrence) dates from 1906; corpus linguistics and compiler design both use token for one unit of input.
Vocabulary The fixed list of \(V\) tokens Latin vocabulum, a name, from vocare, to call.
Corpus The text a tokeniser or model is trained on Latin for body: a body of texts.
Vector A list of \(d\) numbers treated as a point or direction Latin vector, a carrier, from vehere, to carry. William Rowan Hamilton introduced the mathematical sense in the 1840s for the part of a quaternion that carries a point from one place to another.
Embedding The vector assigned to a token; also the map that assigns it English embed, to fix firmly in a surrounding mass. In topology an embedding (often spelled imbedding in the mid-twentieth century) is a map that places one space inside another faithfully, as in Hassler Whitney’s 1936 theorem that every smooth \(n\)-dimensional manifold embeds in \(\mathbb R^{2n}\). Machine learning borrowed it for maps of discrete symbols into \(\mathbb R^d\); the survey by Almeida and Xexéo dates the phrase word embedding in this sense to Morin and Bengio in 2005.
Byte-pair encoding The merge-based tokeniser Philip Gage’s compression scheme, published in The C Users Journal in February 1994: a frequent pair of bytes is encoded as one new byte.
Distributional hypothesis Similar contexts imply similar meanings Zellig Harris, “Distributional Structure”, 1954.
Logit An unnormalised score \(z_v\) before the softmax Joseph Berkson, 1944, as the logistic counterpart of the probit (Chester Bliss, 1934, from probability unit).
Softmax \(e^{z_v}/\sum_u e^{z_u}\) John Bridle, 1990: a smooth (“soft”) version of picking the maximum. The function itself is the Boltzmann distribution of statistical mechanics.
Skip-gram Predict the words around a word, skipping over it From n-gram, a run of n words, the unit of the statistical language models that go back to Claude Shannon’s 1948 paper on information theory.
Transformer The attention-based architecture Named by Vaswani and colleagues, 2017, for transforming one sequence of vectors into another.

Method and sources

Tokeniser. Classic BPE (Sennrich and colleagues’ variant, with an end-of-word marker) on the 253,854 distinct words of text8, weighted by count, with incremental pair-count updates; ties broken by the pair’s characters. The page holds the first 8,000 merges and applies them by rank, as the learned tokeniser would. The GPT-2 and GPT-4o tokenisers are the r50k_base and o200k_base encodings, loaded in your browser from the gpt-tokenizer package.

word2vec. gensim 4.4, skip-gram, 100 dimensions, window 5, 5 negative samples, subsampling threshold \(10^{-3}\), minimum count 5, 5 epochs, seed 1. For the PMI comparison, co-occurrences among the 3,000 most frequent words were counted with gensim’s effective weighting: a context at distance \(\delta\) in a window of 5 counts with weight \((6-\delta)/5\) (its dynamic window), multiplied by the probability that subsampling keeps each word. \(\#(w)\) is the weighted count of all pairs with centre \(w\), \(P_n\) is the unigram distribution to the \(\frac34\) power, and the dot products use the input vectors and gensim’s output (syn1neg) vectors. Analogy accuracy uses gensim’s evaluate_word_analogies with the 30,000 most frequent words.

GloVe. The 100-dimensional glove-wiki-gigaword-100 vectors (Wikipedia 2014 and Gigaword 5, 6 billion tokens), via the gensim-data repository. Analogy accuracy over the 100,000 most frequent words, with and without excluding the three input words; the page uses the 20,000 most frequent words, unit-normalised and quantised to 8 bits.

GPT-2. The openai-community/gpt2 weights from Hugging Face (124M parameters). Norms and cosines are computed over all 50,257 rows of wte; the mean direction is the normalised mean of the unit-normalised rows. Neighbours by cosine. Principal components of wpe after centring.
