Stellar Nucleosynthesis

How stars make the elements, and Fred Hoyle’s carbon resonance

From Gamow tunnelling to the triple-alpha process: the mathematics of thermonuclear reaction rates, the pp chain and CNO cycle, and the story of Fred Hoyle, the Hoyle state and the B2FH paper.

Almost every atom heavier than helium was made inside a star. The carbon in your cells, the oxygen you breathe and the iron in your blood were assembled by thermonuclear reactions in stellar cores and then scattered by stellar winds and supernovae. This post covers the physics that makes that possible, in three steps: why fusion happens at all at stellar temperatures, how the rates of the main reaction chains depend on temperature, and how Fred Hoyle used the existence of carbon-based life to predict a property of the carbon-12 nucleus before anyone had measured it.

A short history

Why do stars shine? In 1920 Arthur Eddington argued that stars are powered by turning hydrogen into helium. Four hydrogen nuclei weigh about 0.7% more than one helium nucleus, so the conversion releases energy through \(E = mc^2\). The objection was that stellar cores seemed far too cool: at roughly \(10^7\) K, protons do not have nearly enough energy to climb the Coulomb barrier. The way out came with quantum mechanics. In 1928 George Gamow showed that nuclei can tunnel through the barrier, and by the late 1930s Hans Bethe (and independently Carl von Weizsäcker) had worked out the reaction chains that power ordinary stars. Bethe’s 1939 paper laid out the proton–proton chain and the carbon–nitrogen–oxygen cycle.

Where do the heavier elements come from? In the 1940s there were two rival ideas. George Gamow and collaborators (Alpher, Bethe, Gamow, 1948) proposed that all the elements were cooked in the first minutes of the Big Bang. That runs into a wall: there are no stable nuclei with mass number 5 or 8, so building up from helium one nucleon at a time stalls, and the Big Bang makes essentially only hydrogen, helium and traces of lithium.

Enter Fred Hoyle. Fred Hoyle (1915–2001), a Cambridge astronomer, argued in 1946 that the heavier elements are built inside stars as they evolve. He was, with Hermann Bondi and Thomas Gold, also the author of the steady-state cosmology (1948), a rival to the Big Bang picture. The phrase “Big Bang” itself is usually credited to Hoyle, from a 1949 BBC radio talk. He later said it was meant as a vivid image and not a sneer, though the claim is still debated. Whatever his view of cosmology, his work on nucleosynthesis was right, and it outlived the steady-state theory.

The bottleneck was carbon. Stars can make helium, but to get anything heavier they must fuse helium nuclei (alpha particles). Two alphas make beryllium-8, which is unbound and falls apart in about \(10^{-16}\) s. A third alpha has to arrive within that window. Hoyle realised that for this to work at a useful rate in stellar cores, carbon-12 must have an excited state at roughly 7.65 MeV that lines up with the energy of the three-alpha system. In 1953 he pressed this on Willy Fowler’s group at Caltech. Fowler’s team (Dunbar, Cook, Whaling and colleagues) measured it, and found a \(0^+\) state at about 7.65 MeV, now called the Hoyle state. Hoyle published the full programme in 1954.

In 1957 Margaret Burbidge, Geoffrey Burbidge, Willy Fowler and Fred Hoyle published “Synthesis of the Elements in Stars”, universally known as B2FH. It laid out the processes of nucleosynthesis (hydrogen burning, helium burning, the alpha process, the s- and r-processes of neutron capture, and others) and connected them to observed abundances. Fowler shared the 1983 Nobel Prize in Physics for this work (with Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar, who was honoured for other research); Hoyle was not included, which many in the field regard as a serious omission.

There is also a famous gloss on the Hoyle state: that Hoyle predicted it because carbon-based life exists, as an early anthropic argument. Historians disagree about how much of that reasoning was in Hoyle’s mind in 1953 versus added in later retellings. The nuclear physics is on firmer ground than the narrative.

Why fusion happens at all: Gamow tunnelling

Two nuclei with charges \(Z_1 e\) and \(Z_2 e\) repel each other with Coulomb energy \(Z_1 Z_2 e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r)\). At the nuclear radius (a few femtometres) this barrier is of order an MeV, while the thermal energy at the Sun’s core temperature of \(1.5\times10^7\) K is only \(kT \approx 1.3\) keV. Classical collisions would almost never get through.

Quantum tunnelling gives a small but non-zero probability. For relative energy \(E\) much below the barrier height, the penetration probability is approximately

\[ P(E) \approx \exp\!\left(-\sqrt{E_G/E}\right), \qquad E_G = 2 m_r c^2 \,(\pi \alpha Z_1 Z_2)^2, \]

where \(m_r\) is the reduced mass, \(\alpha \approx 1/137\) is the fine-structure constant and \(E_G\) is the Gamow energy. It grows rapidly with the charges, which is why hydrogen burns first and heavier fuels need higher temperatures.

reactions <- tribble(

  "alpha + alpha",    2,   2,   4,   4, 1.0e8,
  "alpha + 12C",      2,   6,   4,  12, 2.0e8
) |>
  mutate(E_G_keV = gamow_energy(Z1, Z2, A1, A2))

The Gamow peak

Particles in a hot plasma follow a Maxwell–Boltzmann energy distribution, \(\propto \exp(-E/kT)\), which favours low energies. Tunnelling probability favours high energies. The reaction rate per particle pair is the integral of their product,

\[ \langle\sigma v\rangle = \left(\frac{8}{\pi m_r}\right)^{1/2}\frac{1}{(kT)^{3/2}} \int_0^\infty S(E)\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{E}{kT}-\sqrt{\frac{E_G}{E}}\right)dE, \]

where the cross-section is written as \(\sigma(E) = \dfrac{S(E)}{E}\,e^{-\sqrt{E_G/E}}\) and the astrophysical S-factor \(S(E)\) absorbs the slowly varying nuclear physics. The exponent is minimised at the Gamow peak

\[ E_0 = \left(\frac{E_G\,(kT)^2}{4}\right)^{1/3}, \qquad \Delta = \frac{4}{\sqrt{3}}\sqrt{E_0\,kT}, \]

with \(\Delta\) the \(1/e\) full width. For protons in the Sun’s core the peak sits at about 6 keV, many times the mean thermal energy of \(\sim 1.3\) keV, and only a thin slice of the distribution is doing any fusing.

T_sun <- 1.5e7
EG_pp <- gamow_energy(1, 1, 1, 1)
kT <- kT_keV(T_sun)
E <- seq(0.05, 20, length.out = 800)
mb  <- exp(-E / kT)
tun <- exp(-sqrt(EG_pp / E))
prod <- mb * tun
E0 <- (EG_pp * kT^2 / 4)^(1/3)

df <- tibble(
  E = E,
  `Maxwell–Boltzmann` = mb / max(mb),
  `Tunnelling` = tun / max(tun),
  `Product (Gamow peak)` = prod / max(prod)
) |> tidyr::pivot_longer(-E, names_to = "factor", values_to = "value")

ggplot(df, aes(E, value, colour = factor)) +

  geom_vline(xintercept = E0, linetype = "dashed", colour = "grey40") +

How steeply do rates depend on temperature?

Near its maximum, the integrand is approximately a Gaussian, and the integral gives a rate that depends on temperature as \(\langle\sigma v\rangle \propto T^{\,n}\) with the local exponent

\[ n = \frac{\tau - 2}{3}, \qquad \tau = \frac{3E_0}{kT} = 3\left(\frac{E_G}{4\,kT}\right)^{1/3}. \]

This exponent explains why different stars burn different fuels in different regimes. Reactions with a larger Coulomb barrier have a larger \(\tau\), so they respond far more violently to temperature.

reactions |>
  mutate(
    tau = 3 * (E_G_keV / (4 * kT_keV(T)))^(1/3),
    n   = (tau - 2) / 3
  ) |>
  transmute(reaction, T, E_G_keV = round(E_G_keV), tau = round(tau, 1), n = round(n, 1))
# A tibble: 4 × 5
  reaction              T E_G_keV   tau     n
  <chr>             <dbl>   <dbl> <dbl> <dbl>
1 p + p          15000000     490  13.7   3.9
2 p + 14N        20000000   44779  56    18  
3 alpha + alpha 100000000   31332  29.1   9  
4 alpha + 12C   200000000  422984  54.9  17.6

The alpha + alpha row is the non-resonant value; the resonant triple-alpha process below is far steeper. For p + p at \(1.5\times10^7\) K the rate goes roughly as \(T^4\); for proton capture on nitrogen-14 it goes as a much higher power of \(T\). This is why the CNO cycle dominates over the pp chain in stars somewhat more massive than the Sun. It also shows up in stellar structure: a CNO-burning core has a steep temperature gradient, which drives convection.

grid <- expand.grid(T = 10^seq(6.8, 8.6, length.out = 200), i = seq_len(nrow(reactions))) |>

    reaction = reactions$reaction[i],
    EG = reactions$E_G_keV[i],
    n = (3 * (EG / (4 * kT_keV(T)))^(1/3) - 2) / 3
  )

Hydrogen burning: pp chain and CNO cycle

Both routes convert four protons into one helium-4 nucleus:

\[ 4\,{}^{1}\mathrm{H} \;\to\; {}^{4}\mathrm{He} + 2e^{+} + 2\nu_e + \text{energy}. \]

The energy released is the mass difference. Using atomic masses, \(m({}^1\mathrm{H}) = 1.007825\) u and \(m({}^4\mathrm{He}) = 4.002603\) u,

m_H1  <- 1.007825
m_He4 <- 4.002603
Q_MeV    <- (4 * m_H1 - m_He4) * amu_MeV
frac     <- (4 * m_H1 - m_He4) / (4 * m_H1)

# Sun: how long could hydrogen burning last?
L_sun <- 3.828e26; M_sun <- 1.989e30; c_ms <- 2.998e8; yr <- 3.156e7
t_ms <- 0.1 * frac * M_sun * c_ms^2 / L_sun / yr    # ~10% of mass burns on the main sequence
c(Q_MeV = Q_MeV, mass_fraction_released = frac, main_sequence_years = t_ms)
                 Q_MeV mass_fraction_released    main_sequence_years 
          2.673108e+01           7.118547e-03           1.053369e+10

That is about 26.7 MeV per helium nucleus (a bit less reaches the star, since neutrinos carry some away), or 0.71% of the rest mass. Assuming the Sun burns about 10% of its hydrogen at its current luminosity, that gives a lifetime of roughly 11 billion years, in line with the Sun’s expected main-sequence life.

The pp chain begins with the slowest, weak-interaction step:

\[ p + p \to {}^{2}\mathrm{H} + e^{+} + \nu_e , \]

which is the bottleneck, with a characteristic time of billions of years per proton. This is why the Sun lasts so long. Deuterium then captures another proton, \({}^2\mathrm{H}(p,\gamma){}^3\mathrm{He}\), and two \({}^3\mathrm{He}\) nuclei combine to give \({}^4\mathrm{He}\) plus two protons (the dominant branch).

The CNO cycle uses carbon, nitrogen and oxygen as catalysts, never consumed overall. The slowest step is \({}^{14}\mathrm{N}(p,\gamma){}^{15}\mathrm{O}\), which sets the cycle’s overall speed and, given the high Coulomb barrier, its steep temperature dependence.

A reaction network, in general

The abundance of each species \(i\) (written as a number fraction \(Y_i = n_i/(\rho N_A)\)) obeys a coupled system of ordinary differential equations. For a reaction \(a + b \to c + \dots\),

\[ \frac{dY_i}{dt} = \sum_{j,k} \frac{\rho N_A \langle\sigma v\rangle_{jk}}{1+\delta_{jk}}\,Y_j Y_k\,(\pm\text{stoichiometry}) \;+\; \text{decays}. \]

Real codes solve hundreds of such equations simultaneously. The system is stiff: the fastest and slowest rates can differ by tens of orders of magnitude, so implicit integrators are needed.

Helium burning and the triple-alpha process

Once the core runs out of hydrogen it contracts and heats up. At around \(10^8\) K, helium can fuse. The reaction has to proceed through two steps:

\[ \alpha + \alpha \;\rightleftharpoons\; {}^{8}\mathrm{Be}, \qquad {}^{8}\mathrm{Be} + \alpha \;\to\; {}^{12}\mathrm{C}^{*} \;\to\; {}^{12}\mathrm{C} + \gamma\ (\text{or } e^{+}e^{-}). \]

\({}^8\mathrm{Be}\) lies only about 92 keV above two free alphas and lives for roughly \(10^{-16}\) s. At stellar temperatures a small equilibrium population of it nonetheless exists, given (up to order-unity factors) by the Saha equation,

\[ \frac{n_{\mathrm{Be}}}{n_\alpha^2} \sim \left(\frac{2\pi\hbar^2}{M_\alpha kT}\right)^{3/2} e^{-E_{\mathrm{Be}}/kT}, \qquad E_{\mathrm{Be}} \approx 92\ \text{keV}. \]

At \(10^8\) K (\(kT \approx 8.6\) keV) the Boltzmann factor is only about \(e^{-10.7}\), so beryllium is rare, but not negligibly so. A third alpha capturing on this beryllium would normally be too weak to matter. The Hoyle state, however, sits just above the \({}^8\mathrm{Be} + \alpha\) threshold: at 7.654 MeV excitation energy in \({}^{12}\mathrm{C}\), it is about 0.287 MeV above the \({}^8\mathrm{Be}+\alpha\) threshold, or 0.379 MeV above the three-alpha threshold. Capture into it is a resonance, so the cross-section is enormously boosted.

For a narrow resonance, the resulting three-body rate per unit volume is

\[ r_{3\alpha} = 3^{3/2}\, n_\alpha^3 \left(\frac{2\pi\hbar^2}{M_\alpha kT}\right)^{3}\frac{\Gamma}{\hbar}\, \exp\!\left(-\frac{Q}{kT}\right), \]

with \(Q = 379.5\) keV the resonance energy above the three-alpha threshold and \(\Gamma\) the effective width. Most Hoyle-state nuclei fall apart again into \(^8\)Be + \(\alpha\); the quantity that matters is the small radiative width \(\Gamma_\gamma \approx 3.7\) meV (plus a smaller pair-emission contribution), because only decays by radiation or pair emission produce stable carbon.

hbar  <- 1.054572e-34      # J s
kB    <- 1.380649e-23      # J/K
M_a   <- 4.001506 * 1.66054e-27   # kg
eV    <- 1.602177e-19
N_A   <- 6.02214e23
Q_J   <- 379.5e3 * eV      # resonance energy above 3-alpha threshold
Gam_J <- 3.7e-3 * eV       # effective width ~ radiative width

# rate per unit volume (m^-3 s^-1); rho in g/cm^3, pure helium
r3a <- function(T, rho) {
  n_a <- (rho * 1e3 / 4.0026e-3) * N_A            # alphas per m^3 (rho in kg/m^3 / molar mass)
  thermal <- 2 * pi * hbar^2 / (M_a * kB * T)
  3^1.5 * n_a^3 * thermal^3 * (Gam_J / hbar) * exp(-Q_J / (kB * T))
}

# alpha-particle lifetime against destruction: n_alpha / (3 r)
tau_alpha_yr <- function(T, rho) {
  n_a <- (rho * 1e3 / 4.0026e-3) * N_A
  n_a / (3 * r3a(T, rho)) / yr
}

tau_demo <- tau_alpha_yr(1e8, 1e5)

# format a number as LaTeX scientific notation for inline use
sci_tex <- function(x, digits = 2) {
  e <- floor(log10(x)); m <- signif(x / 10^e, digits)
  sprintf("%s \\times 10^{%d}", format(m), e)
}

At \(T = 10^8\) K and \(\rho = 10^5\) g cm\(^{-3}\) (the sort of dense, degenerate core in which helium ignites in a low-mass star), this simple estimate gives a helium lifetime of roughly \(4.8 \times 10^{7}\) years. At the same temperature but \(10^3\) g cm\(^{-3}\) it would be \(10^4\) times longer, because the rate scales as \(\rho^3\) while the helium supply scales as \(\rho\). Helium burns only when the core is both hot and dense.

ta <- expand.grid(T = 10^seq(7.8, 8.6, length.out = 200), rho = c(1e4, 1e5, 1e6)) |>
  mutate(tau = tau_alpha_yr(T, rho), rho = factor(format(rho, scientific = FALSE, big.mark = ",")))

ggplot(ta, aes(T, tau, colour = rho)) +
  geom_line(linewidth = 1) +
  scale_x_log10(breaks = c(1e8, 2e8, 4e8), labels = scales::label_scientific(digits = 1)) +
  scale_y_log10(labels = scales::label_log()) +
  labs(x = "Temperature (K)", y = "Helium lifetime (years)",
       colour = expression(rho~"(g/cm"^3*")")) +
  theme(legend.position = "top")

The local exponent of this rate is enormous. Since \(r \propto T^{-3}e^{-Q/kT}\) (the \(T^{-3}\) coming from the thermal factor),

\[ \frac{d\ln r_{3\alpha}}{d\ln T} = \frac{Q}{kT} - 3. \]

Texp <- 1e8
n_ta <- Q_J / (kB * Texp) - 3
n_ta
[1] 41.03916

At \(10^8\) K that is 41, so the rate scales as roughly \(T^{41}\). A 10% temperature rise speeds helium burning up by a factor of about 50. Helium ignition therefore happens abruptly, and in stars with degenerate cores it is explosive (the helium flash).

What if the resonance were somewhere else?

The factor \(\exp(-Q/kT)\) makes the rate exponentially sensitive to the resonance energy. If the Hoyle state were shifted by \(\Delta E\) relative to its real position, the rate at fixed temperature would change by \(\exp(-\Delta E/kT)\) (holding all else fixed, which is an idealisation).

kT100 <- kT_keV(1e8)
shift <- tibble(dE = seq(-150, 150, length.out = 300)) |>
  mutate(rel = exp(-dE / kT100))

ggplot(shift, aes(dE, rel)) +
  geom_line(linewidth = 1, colour = "#2c7fb8") +
  geom_vline(xintercept = 0, linetype = "dashed", colour = "grey50") +
  scale_y_log10(labels = scales::label_log()) +
  labs(x = "Resonance shift ΔE (keV)", y = "Rate relative to actual")

This sensitivity is what gives the “fine-tuning” discussions their punch. Fairer treatments, though, vary underlying nuclear parameters and track how the resonance energy, the beryllium state and the later burning of carbon into oxygen all respond together. The conclusion that comes out of such studies is more nuanced than “the universe is exactly balanced”, and what to make of it is a matter of ongoing debate rather than a result.

After helium: carbon, oxygen and beyond

Once carbon exists, it can capture another alpha to form oxygen, \({}^{12}\mathrm{C}(\alpha,\gamma){}^{16}\mathrm{O}\). The competition between this and the triple-alpha process sets the carbon-to-oxygen ratio at the end of helium burning, which controls what a star does afterwards. That rate is known only to limited accuracy, and measuring it more precisely is still an active problem in nuclear astrophysics.

In more massive stars the sequence continues in shells and cores: carbon burning (\(\sim 6\times10^8\) K), neon, oxygen and silicon burning, each stage faster and hotter than the last. The chain stops at iron-group nuclei because they have the greatest binding energy per nucleon, so fusing them no longer releases energy. Elements beyond iron are mostly made by neutron capture: slowly (the s-process, in evolved stars) or rapidly (the r-process, in neutron-star mergers and perhaps some supernovae).

Takeaways

  • Fusion in stars depends on quantum tunnelling. The reaction rate is the product of two competing exponentials, giving the narrow Gamow peak.
  • The temperature sensitivity of a reaction, \(n = (\tau-2)/3\), is set by the Coulomb barrier through the Gamow energy.
  • Hydrogen burning releases about 26.7 MeV per helium nucleus, enough for the Sun to shine for billions of years.
  • The triple-alpha process needs a resonance, the Hoyle state, to make carbon at a useful rate. Fred Hoyle inferred its existence in 1953, and Fowler’s group confirmed it.
  • B2FH (1957) put stellar nucleosynthesis on a systematic footing. Fowler received the Nobel Prize in 1983; Hoyle did not.
